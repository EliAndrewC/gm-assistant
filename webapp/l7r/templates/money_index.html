What the records leave out

Most of these figures come from account books, wage ledgers, servants' contracts and tax registers: the records of money changing hands, kept by merchants, officials and lords. Work that was never paid for left almost no trace in them, and the largest share of that work was women's. Spinning, weaving, sewing and mending a household's clothes, cooking, preserving food, hauling water and raising children took up most of a woman's days, and almost nobody at the time wrote down how many. Where a figure here depends on that work, it is thin or missing for that reason.

This is not a gap in one source but a fact about the past itself. The lives of lords, officials and soldiers are recorded far better than the lives of common people, and men's lives better than women's. Every reconstruction of a pre-modern society, and every setting built from one, sees most clearly what the people who kept the records thought worth counting. Reading Rokugan's prices well means remembering who is missing from them. What the research found, and how historians read around the silence.
