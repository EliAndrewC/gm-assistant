What's a koku worth?

A koku is a mass of rice. What it buys today depends on the good: a koku's rice buys far more cloth than housing, now that cloth is cheap and rooms are dear. So there is no single answer, only a range.

Which periods set Rokugan's prices

Each good's Rokugani price is the weighted mean of its price in rice in every period that records both. Every period weighs 1: China counts as much as Japan, since Rokugan's lands are more like China's, and Meiji Tokyo counts for its food, though not for the kerosene and linen foreign trade brought. Set a period to 0 to leave it out, or higher to count it more. A period counts only for the goods it records beside rice, since rice is the yardstick every other price is measured in.

How the koku is reckoned

A koku is about {{ '%.1f' % reading.koku_kg }} kg of rice, and Rokugan's coins count in it: 1,000 sen to the koku. Every other good's Rokugani price is how much rice it cost, averaged over every period the research records it in, {{ model.eras|length }} periods of Japan and China from the 1490s to the 1910s. Counting in rice rather than coin takes out each period's own money, so a debased coin or a dear decade of silver drops out. Open a good above for the periods behind its price and the research each comes from.

Take the amount of a good a koku's rice bought, price that amount today, and you have what a koku buys of that good alone: about {{ dollars(rice_alone) }} of rice, but under {{ dollars(50) }} of cloth and thousands of dollars of housing. Those spread over two orders of magnitude, so a koku's worth depends on how they are averaged. The year of necessities (the whole basket) answers the household's question, what a year of necessities cost then and costs now; it is the same as the average weighted by spending, and the high end, because the goods that became dear (above all a room) weigh heavily in it. The average over goods takes the geometric mean by default, which no single good can dominate; it is the central figure the money skill quotes. Economic historians use these baskets mainly to compare a laborer's wage with the cost of living across places and centuries, which is what wage dollars do.

The default basket is Bassino & Ma's published Japanese basket (food, cloth, lamp oil), with salt, fuel and a share of a room added because a household cannot live without them. Some of Kyoto 1745's prices are Bassino & Ma's reconstructions from Meiji price ratios, so they are not counted again beside Tokyo 1882; the room's price is still a guess.
