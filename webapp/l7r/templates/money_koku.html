How the koku is reckoned

Rokugan's coins are pegged to rice: a koku is about {{ '%.1f' % reading.koku_kg }} kg of it, and in Kyoto in 1745-54 a kilogram of rice cost {{ '%.2f' % model.rice_ref }} g of pure silver (in the Genbun silver of the day, 1.725 g to the monme), so a koku is worth about {{ (model.rice_ref * reading.koku_kg)|round|int }} g. Every good in the basket has a price in that silver and a price in today's dollars. The basket's dollars divided by its silver says what a gram of that silver buys today, and a koku's worth of it is the goods-dollar figure.

That makes the figure a ratio: a good that is dear today but was cheap in Kyoto (fish, a room) pushes it up, and one that is cheap today pulls it down. The standard basket is Bassino & Ma's published Japanese basket (food, cloth, lamp oil), with salt, fuel and a share of a room added because a household cannot live without them. Kyoto's own books price the salt and the firewood; no Kyoto or Osaka rent was found, so the room's price is still a guess, borrowed from Edo decades later. Sources and reasoning.
