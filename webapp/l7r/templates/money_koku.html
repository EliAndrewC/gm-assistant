What's a koku worth?

A koku is a mass of rice. What it buys today depends on the good: a koku's silver buys far more cloth than housing, now that cloth is cheap and rooms are dear. So there is no single answer, only a range.

Which periods set Rokugan's prices

Each good's Rokugani price is the weighted mean of its price in rice in every period that records both, so a debased coin or a dear decade of silver drops out. Every period weighs 1 for now, the Chinese and Meiji ones included; set a period to 0 to leave it out, or higher to count it more. Rice itself is the anchor and does not move.

How the koku is reckoned

Rokugan's coins are pegged to rice: a koku is about {{ '%.1f' % reading.koku_kg }} kg of it, and in Kyoto in 1745-54 a kilogram of rice cost {{ '%.2f' % model.rice_ref }} g of pure silver (the Genbun silver of the day), so a koku is about {{ (model.rice_ref * reading.koku_kg)|round|int }} g. Every other good's Rokugani price is its price in rice, averaged over the periods the research records, times that anchor.

Divide a good's price today by its price in Rokugan and multiply by a koku's silver, and you have what a koku buys of that good alone: about {{ dollars(rice_alone) }} of rice, but under {{ dollars(50) }} of cloth and thousands of dollars of housing. Those spread over two orders of magnitude, so a koku's worth depends on how they are averaged. The whole basket answers the household's question, what a year of necessities cost then and costs now; it is the same as the average weighted by spending, and the high end, because the goods that became dear (above all a room) weigh heavily in it. The average over goods takes the geometric mean by default, which no single good can dominate; it is the central figure the money skill quotes. Economic historians use these baskets mainly to compare a laborer's wage with the cost of living across places and centuries, which is what wage dollars do.

The standard basket is Bassino & Ma's published Japanese basket (food, cloth, lamp oil), with salt, fuel and a share of a room added because a household cannot live without them. Some of Kyoto 1745's prices are Bassino & Ma's reconstructions from Meiji price ratios, so they are not counted again beside Tokyo 1882; the room's price is still a guess. Which modern goods match the historical ones, and how historians average them, is being researched (diagram#17). Sources and reasoning.
