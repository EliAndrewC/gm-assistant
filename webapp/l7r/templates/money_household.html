Money

Work that was never paid

Cloth, food, fuel, straw goods, repairs and care: what a household did for itself, how long it took, and why so much of it went unrecorded.

織

The rest of this section prices a koku by what it buys and by the paid days it stands for. Neither sees the work a household did for itself, though that work made much of what a family wore, ate and burned. A robe woven at home cost no coin, only someone's days.

Can the sources count that work, class by class? Not fully. They give one farm's year counted task by task, the days a household spent on its own cloth, who did each task, and what a city household bought instead. They do not give hours for each member of a household, or any count at all for a city household's own work. Each figure below says how solid it is.

Most of this work was women's, and women's household work was almost never written down at the time. That is why so many figures below read "not recorded", and the silence is a historical fact in its own right: why so much is missing.

So the answer turns on the fiber. Where a household spun cotton, its cloth was a few weeks' work a year; where it made hemp or ramie cloth, as in the centuries before cotton, the same clothes may well have been the largest claim on one woman's year.

The gap between the market price and the days' worth is the cotton and the weaver's skill; what ginned cotton cost has not been researched yet.

Why so much is missing

Women's household work was almost never written down at the time. That is why so many figures on this page read "not recorded", and it is a fact about the past in its own right, not a gap in one source. Paid days and prices were recorded because someone paid them; work done at home for the household was not. {{ badge({'verdict': 'ATTESTED', 'source': 'records'}) }}

  • Writing served rule, and the rulers were men. A historian of Edo-period women puts the biggest cause in the sources themselves: writing spread because rulers needed it to govern, the samurai who ruled were all men, and the village officials who kept the village's papers were men too. What survives is administrative and economic records, full of the names of male household heads.
  • Historians recover women's work from records kept for other purposes: village headmen's diaries, temple population registers, rules written for women (read against their purpose), and official commendations of exemplary women.
  • In China the same shadow falls differently. While cloth was a tax, women's weaving was tied to the state; when the state stopped asking for bolts of cloth, their work was, in one historian's phrase, masked, not lessened. The best estimates of Chinese farm and household labor rest on a narrow base of documents, and the gazetteers' lives of women were written to teach virtue, not to count days.

The same is true of every pre-modern society, and of any setting built from one: the lives of lords and soldiers are recorded far better than those of common people, and men's better than women's. Reconstructing a household's year means reading around that silence, and saying where it is. More on the Money page.

The proverb

The Money page quotes a Rokugani farming proverb: No one who rises before dawn on all three hundred and sixty days of the year will fail to make their family rich. No Chinese or Japanese source gives it in that form; the English wording traces to Malcolm Gladwell's Outliers, which calls it a Chinese proverb. {{ badge({'verdict': 'SILENT', 'source': 'proverb'}) }}

Its halves are old. 早起三朝當一工, zǎo qǐ sān zhāo dāng yī gōng, "rising early three mornings makes one day's work": the first line of a joking poem by Lou Yue, who died in 1213. A farming almanac begun in 1314 sets it among sayings for those who farm, beside "a lifetime's plan lies in diligence" and "if you want wealth and honor, you must put in bitter effort". {{ badge({'verdict': 'ATTESTED', 'source': 'proverb'}) }}
