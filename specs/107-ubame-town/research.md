## 1. The refining forge (ōkaji / Chinese *chao* fining)

### What the research found - CHINA FIRST

Ming ironworks converted blast-furnace pig iron to wrought iron by **fining**, Chinese *chao* 炒,
literally "stir-frying." The fining hearth was an **open fire under a forced blast, fuelled with
charcoal**: wood, charcoal and broken cast iron were charged in and ignited, air was pumped, and
when the iron went semi-molten it was stirred with an iron rod under the blast. Song Yingxing
describes a rectangular hearth (*tang*) set "a few *chi* away and a few *cun* lower" than the
blast-furnace outlet, with workers standing on a protective wall above it, stirring the melt with
willow poles (which burned down 2-3 *cun* per cycle) while spreading a mineral additive
(*wuchaoni*). The practice is far older than the Ming - a cast-iron smelting **and fining** site of
the Eastern Han is excavated at Xuxiebian in Sichuan.

Two Chinese facts about **scale and fuel** matter here. A large Ming ironworks is recorded with
**200 furnace-tenders, 300 miners and 200 charcoal producers**, plus 200 oxen and 50 river vessels:
the fuel workforce is as large as the furnace workforce. And in the Northern Song the iron industry
ran on charcoal until it had deforested large parts of north China - output reached roughly
125,000 tons a year by 1078, about six times the ~800 CE level - after which coal largely replaced
charcoal by the end of the 11th century. Song iron-smelting households were **government-regulated**,
with state support that explicitly included **charcoal supplies**.

### What the research found - JAPAN CORROBORATING

Japan's tatara evolved into a box furnace whose chief product was molten pig iron (*zuku*), and
that drove the invention in the **17th century** of a **two-stage refining process** to convert it
to wrought iron: the **ōkaji** 大鍛冶. The *kera* from the tatara went first to a workshop called
the *dōba*, where it was crushed and sorted; the lower-quality fractions (*zuku* and *bukera*) then
went to the **ōkajiba**, where decarburization produced flat bars called **wari-tetsu** 割鉄.
"Zuku-making by Tatara and refining by Ōkaji formed the Japanese indirect wrought-iron making
system" - two operations, two sites. At their 18th-19th century peak the ironworks of the **Chugoku
Mountains produced 80% of Japan's iron**.

### Does the design match? - PARTLY, WITH ONE DISCLOSED DIVERGENCE

The design matches on everything that gets drawn: an **open-sided** hearth shed rather than a closed
building (the fining hearth is an open fire and must vent), **charcoal** as the fuel with its own
store on the premises, **two hearths** (the two-stage refining is documented on both sides), a
stirring/working floor, and **flat bar iron** as the output - which is precisely the "bar iron" the
existing Mode A magistracy sheet already tallies and seals.

**The divergence, stated plainly**: the *Chinese* arrangement puts the fining hearth a few feet from
the blast-furnace outlet so the iron runs in still molten. Ubame cannot do that, because the GM's
canon fixes the smelting furnaces out at the fuel in the hills and the town 15 miles from the
treeline. So Ubame follows the **Japanese** two-site pattern: cold pig comes down to a separate
valley forge and is re-melted.

This is a legitimate resolution rather than a fudge, and the reason is economic, not aesthetic. The
Chinese single-site arrangement is viable exactly when ore, fuel and a workforce of hundreds are
assembled at one state-supervised complex - which is what the 200/300/200 staffing describes. Ubame
is not that: it is a county-scale trade strung along dispersed oak stands, whose fuel is precisely
what cannot be concentrated (a kiln reduces six parts wood to one of charcoal, so the kiln goes to
the wood). **Dispersed fuel forces two sites.** Per CLAUDE.md's China-first rule, GM setting canon
overrides the historical default, and here the canon and the Japanese practice agree.

### What DETERMINES it in reality

**Economy and topography.** Whether smelting and refining share a site is decided by whether fuel,
ore and labor can be concentrated. Where they can (state ironworks on a navigable river), one site;
where the fuel is dispersed through hill forest (Chugoku, and Ubame), two sites, with the smelter at
the fuel and the refinery in the valley settlement where the carts, the market and the tally are.

### Design consequences taken

- Drawn as an **open-sided shed** (three walls, open working front), never a closed hall.
- **Two hearths** under it with the blast position between them - the two-stage process, drawn.
- Its **own charcoal store** on the premises, because the fuel is the input the process consumes most of.
- **Slag heap** and **stacked bar iron** outside: the waste and the product, which is what tells a reader this is a refinery and not a smithy.
- Sited **off the housing and downwind** (below), because it is a live open fire under forced blast.

## 2. The charcoal yard

### What the research found

**China first.** Charcoal was an industrial input at state scale (above), supplied to regulated
smelting households under government oversight. The Song exhausted north China's fuel supplying it.
So a **charcoal store as a supervised, tallied commodity depot** - not merely a shop's back room - is
the Chinese fact, and it is exactly the relationship the Mode A magistracy sheet already draws
(charcoal "sealed here, never owned").

**Japan corroborating.** *Ton'ya* / *toiya* were the wholesaler-warehousemen of the Edo economy, and
because urban wooden housing burned so readily, fire-resistant stores were built of stone or behind
mortar walls. Edo's serious fires cluster in **winter, when charcoal braziers were in use**.

**The decisive physical finding - charcoal self-heats.** Freshly produced charcoal absorbs oxygen
from the air rapidly enough to heat itself to ignition. **Tightly packed masses of charcoal FINES
are the worst case**, along with charcoal carrying high volatiles - which self-ignites below 150 °C.
The documented handling rule is to stand freshly discharged charcoal **in the open, separate from
previously cooled and conditioned stock, for at least 24 hours**; charcoal that has been exposed to
air for **eight days is no longer considered hazardous**. Water-spraying does not reliably prevent
it.

**A finding that turned out to be load-bearing**: the charcoal *hyō* / *tawara* had **no standard
weight** in the traditional Japanese system, unlike rice. (A *kan* is 3.75 kg, but the charcoal bale
itself was indeterminate.)

### Does the design match? - YES, and one finding CHANGED the design

Match on: roofed stacking sheds (charcoal must stay dry - the county's premium good is bought for an
odorless, smokeless burn, per the existing Mode A grounding), a merchant yard on the cart route, and
a fire separation.

**The change.** The first design was "sheds + weighing floor." The self-heating finding says that is
wrong: a real yard cannot put arriving loads straight under cover with the conditioned stock. So the
yard now draws **an open COOLING APRON, set apart from the covered sheds**, where new loads stand
before they go in. That is the 24-hour/8-day rule made visible, and it is the difference between a
generic warehouse glyph and a charcoal yard.

**The second finding justifies a feature that would otherwise look like decoration.** Because the
charcoal bale had **no standard weight**, charcoal could not be traded by count - it had to be
**weighed at the point of sale**. That is why a weighing floor belongs on the yard at all, and it is
independently why the magistracy's hold on this trade is documentary: the seal on the tally is worth
something precisely because the quantity is not self-evident from the load.

### What DETERMINES it in reality

**The commodity's physics and its measure.** Dryness (value), self-heating (separation and a cooling
ground), and the absence of a standard bale (a weighing floor and a sealed tally). Not town size and
not wealth.

### The fire-gap number, and how it was derived

**30 real feet clear of every other structure.** Reasoned against the ladder this project already
uses rather than invented:

| separation | figure | what it is defending against |
|---|---|---|
| farrier from a stall range (`farrier_keeps_fire_gap`) | ~6 ft | sparks from an attended open forge onto hay |
| **charcoal yard from any structure (new)** | **30 ft** | a self-heating stack that ignites **unattended** |
| refining forge from dwellings (new) | 60 ft | a live worked fire under forced blast, plus noise and smoke |
| crematory / tanning yard from dwellings | 120 ft | putrefaction and smoke carried on the air |

30 ft is about one flame-height clear of a fully-involved 10-12 ft charcoal stack, which is the
usual rule of thumb for radiant ignition of adjacent timber. It sits an order above the attended-forge
figure (the hazard here is that nobody is watching) and well below the nuisance figures (this is
radiant heat over a short distance, not smell over a long one).

## 3. The refining forge's standoff and its downwind siting

**60 real feet from any dwelling**, and on the **downwind** side of the settlement.

The standoff is half the 120 ft nuisance figure and double the charcoal yard's 30 ft, which is the
right ordering: an open fire under a forced blast is a *live* ignition source (worse than a fuel
stack) but it is attended and it does not putrefy (better than a crematory or a tannery). The
Chinese fining hearth is explicitly an open fire under a blast with workers stirring the melt above
it, so the spark and smoke source is not incidental to the process - it *is* the process.

The **downwind** rule keys off the map's existing `windward` knob (default NW, the East Asian winter
monsoon), so the downwind quarter here is the southeast. This is a separate axis from the water
rules: **smoke goes downwind, filth goes downstream.** On this map they point to different corners
(downwind SE, downstream SW), which is a useful property - it is the first pool map where the two
nuisance axes do not collapse into one direction, so it tests that the rules are genuinely separate
rather than accidentally agreeing.

## 4. The drawn clan border

### What the research found

Linear, demarcated borders were **not** foreign to early modern Japan: domains were already building
a territorial order with demarcated boundaries and mutual exclusion, and boundary disputes,
boundary markers and map-making are the evidence. The **Nanbu-Date border mounds** are the worked
example - a series of **earth mounds** marking the ~130 km boundary between Morioka (Nanbu) and
Sendai (Date) domains, re-confirmed by the shogunate in **1642**; "such boundary markers were common
under the Tokugawa shogunate." Separately, the shogunate ordered every province to produce
**kuniezu** 国絵図, provincial land maps, on which those boundaries were drawn.

### Does the design match? - YES, and it resolves a tension the Mode A sheet had already flagged

The Mode A `ubame-magistracy.notes.md` had already noted that "the physically large period border
markers were earthen *mounds*, which is a different thing and not what is drawn," and drew ~3 ft
boundary *pillars* instead as location markers. The Nanbu-Date finding confirms the mounds and
therefore confirms that reservation was right.

The Mode B answer is different in kind and does not conflict: what this map draws is **the line
itself, as a line on a map** - which is exactly what a kuniezu did. A drawn jurisdictional boundary
is an authentic cartographic act, not a physical claim. That is why it must be classified
**overlap-exempt**: the magistracy's east wall standing on the line is the intended arrangement, and
a line of law cannot be "overlapped" by masonry.

### What DETERMINES it in reality

**Jurisdiction, and the need to have agreed it in advance.** A border exists where two authorities
have negotiated and recorded it - which is why the Mode A sheet's parley room, with the line running
across its floor and a pair of kneeling mats on each side, is the architectural expression of the
same fact this line is the cartographic expression of.

## 6. Findings that led to REJECTING something (recorded per Principle XII)

- **Rejected: drawing kilns or a tatara furnace on the town map.** Canon and arithmetic both forbid
  it (six parts wood to one of charcoal, so the kiln goes to the wood), and the Chinese 200-charcoal-
  producers figure shows the fuel workforce lives out at the stands, not in the seat. The hills stay
  off-map.
- **Rejected: the Chinese adjacent-hearth arrangement** for the refining forge - see §1. It would
  have put a blast furnace in the town.
- **Rejected: a covered-only charcoal yard** - see §2. The self-heating rule requires open ground for
  new stock, so the cooling apron is not optional decoration.
- **Rejected: drawing the border as an earthwork.** The period physical marker is a mound, but a
  mound is a structure and would then have to be kept clear of the magistracy's wall - which is the
  opposite of the arrangement the Mode A sheet establishes. The line is drawn as a line.
- **Rejected: reusing the 120 ft nuisance figure for the charcoal yard.** It defends against smell
  carried on air, which is not this hazard; using it would have pushed the yard off the cart route
  that is its whole reason for existing.

**Two defects the automated gate could not see, both found by this pass and fixed:**

1. **The refining forge rendered as a FACE** - two red hearths as eyes, the bellows as a nose, the
   charcoal store's bales as a mouth. Every check was green; only the raster showed it. This is the
   same pareidolia failure that retired the tethered-oxen glyphs (GM 2026-07-25), and the fix is
   structural rather than cosmetic: the hearths and bellows are now ranked in ONE left-weighted row
   along the back wall, the fire draws as a bar banked at the hearth mouth instead of a filled block
   centered in it, and the lower band is deliberately asymmetric. **Nothing on the glyph is mirrored
   about its axis**, which is the general rule the failure teaches.
2. **The windbreak rendered as a round wood dumped in the middle of the town**, hard against the
   flophouse. `village_windbreak_embraces_cluster` tests ADJACENCY, not SHAPE, so it passed. Reshaped
   into a long narrow belt along the farm cluster's windward fringe, which is what a back-village
   belt is.

**One consistency defect between two artifacts of the same place**, also caught here: the manor was
drawn at 250 x 180 ft against its own Mode A sheet's **290 x 200 ft** envelope. Corrected, with the
oak stand pulled north to make room rather than the compound shrunk to fit.

**Nothing in the Phase 0 research was contradicted by the artifact.** The one disclosed divergence
(the Japanese two-site tatara/okaji split rather than the Chinese adjacent-hearth arrangement) is
visible on the sheet exactly as declared: a refinery in the town, and no smelter anywhere.
