**Money research: the work a household did that was never paid, who did it, and how long it took**

Questions from the gm-assistant money skill, asked by the GM (2026-10-26), verbatim (dictated; "Rakugan" = Rokugan,
"fibergrafts" = fibercrafts, both corrected by the GM inline):

> Something that I suspect we need to clarify when it comes to talking about the number of days that we're worked. Is
> the degree to which pre-modern workers were pretty much always working even on days that they were not working for
> wages. Now, admittedly, I do not know the degree to which this was true for members of different castes and classes.
> However, my understanding of farm life, both from members of my own family who grew up on farms and worked on farms,
> and also from what I have read, is that farmers were pretty much always working, even when the weather did not permit
> you to be working the fields. You would be spinning rope or repairing your home or mending your tools, etc. And even
> this does not take into account the fact that a large proportion of clothing was homemade, and that the women of the
> household were always cooking and cleaning and spinning and weaving and sewing and mending, etc. I have heard, though
> I have not seen a source on this, that there is an ancient Chinese proverb that no one who is willing to rise before
> dawn 360 days out of the year will fail to make their family rich. [...] I suspect that this is a farming proverb
> rather than a proverb for educated clerks or caravan guards, etc. Though I actually don't know. I'm also not sure the
> degree to which this would or even could apply to city laborers living in tenement housing, Might not be living in
> the same kinds of households, who might not have the number of personal possessions that a farming homestead
> possesses, and who therefore might not even have the same opportunities to be working all the time. Even once they
> were successful enough to start a family and have a city home for themselves. [...] I think my main question would be
> whether our existing research and our existing sources are good enough to answer for us these questions about how
> many workdays per year of what we might call "home work" existed for the different classes and how this might vary
> between a laborer who has not yet started a family, who is living in tenement housing, versus a laborer who has
> started a family and managed to live in a family or extended family home in the city, etc. And in general, aside from
> the question of what we find or derive or estimate from these sources, it would be useful to have an attempt at a
> more or less exhaustive list of what those "home work" tasks actually are and how time-consuming they were relative
> to one another. For example, my understanding of fiber crafts is such that making clothes was probably the single most
> time-consuming task occurring in a farmhouse as measured by the amount of any one individual's time spent on it, even
> when compared to the actual farming! I do not have actual numbers on this, and My understanding is that the total
> amount of time spent farming by all individuals combined almost certainly exceeded the total amount of time spent on
> clothes making due to the fact that more members of the family participated in farm work than in fibercrafts, But I
> would be interested to see some actual research on this, and then perhaps even a section of our money site that
> discusses this.

And, after the plan below was agreed (verbatim):

> we should explicitly call out the fact that, as you say, women's homework was almost never written down at the time,
> and that this is a reason for some of our numbers being spotty, because this in and of itself is an important
> historical fact to convey about the insight that we have into pre-modern cultures.

Why it matters: the toolkit prices a koku two ways, by what it buys and by the paid working days it stands for. Neither
sees work that was never sold. A homemade robe cost a farm household no coin but weeks of someone's time; a tenement
laborer bought what a farm wife made. The GM wants a Money page on this work: the household's year, a list of the
tasks with their relative cost in time, and homemade goods priced in days of work beside their market prices. Where a
figure is missing because nobody wrote it down, the page will say so, and say why.

### 1. A farm household's year, by member
- **Question**: for a Tokugawa farm household (a Ming/Qing one beside it), how were the days and hours of each member
  (husband, wife, grown children, children, elders, any servant) split between fieldwork, side trades for sale, and
  unpaid work for the household itself, and how did that change by season (the slack winter, rain days, the busy
  transplanting and harvest)?
- **Figure needed**: days or hours a year (or a share of the year) per member and per kind of work, with place and date;
  failing that, the off-season and night tasks a source names.
- **Region order**: Japan first, China beside it.

### 2. The household tasks, listed, with their relative cost in time
- **Question**: as near an exhaustive list as the sources allow of the unpaid work a household did, with who did each
  and how long it took relative to the others. The session's starting list, to be confirmed, corrected or extended:
  fiber (growing or buying hemp or cotton, retting, spinning, weaving, dyeing, sewing, unpicking and washing robes,
  mending); food (hulling and polishing rice, cooking, pickling and preserving, making miso and soy sauce); fuel and
  water (gathering and cutting wood, charcoal, hauling water); straw (rope, sandals, mats, rain capes, bags); the house
  and tools (roof and wall repair, tool mending); care (children, the sick, the old).
- **Figure needed**: hours or days per task per year, or per unit (a meal, a straw sandal, a roof).
- **Specific check, the GM's hypothesis**: was clothing the household's largest single claim on one person's time,
  larger for its maker than fieldwork was for any one worker, while fieldwork, shared by more members, was larger in
  total? Find the figures that confirm or refute either half.
- **Region order**: Japan first, China beside it.

### 3. The fiber chain, in hours
- **Question**: from plant to finished garment, how many hours of work for one ordinary robe (or a bolt, tan, of cloth)
  in hemp, in cotton and in silk: growing, preparing the fiber, spinning, weaving, dyeing, sewing? How many robes or bolts
  did a household make in a year, and how much cloth did it buy?
- **Figure needed**: hours per stage per unit, and a household's yearly output; a measured modern craft rate is acceptable
  as a COMPARATIVE source where no period figure exists.
- **Region order**: Japan first, China beside it, Europe or modern experiment as comparison only.

### 4. City households: what they bought instead of making
- **Question**: how much household work did a city worker do, and how did it differ between a single laborer in a back
  tenement (nagaya) and a laborer or artisan with a family in a house of their own? What did they buy that a farm
  household made (used clothing, cooked food, washing, mending, firewood, water), and what did each cost?
- **Figure needed**: the trades that sold these services and goods, their prices, and any account of a city household's
  unpaid work.
- **Region order**: Japan first (Edo, Osaka, Kyoto), China beside it.

### 5. Other workers' days off
- **Question**: what did a live-in servant, a merchant house's clerk, a guard or a porter do on days not worked for pay:
  rest, their own household's work, or a side trade?
- **Figure needed**: any account, with place and date.

### 6. A proverb
- **Question**: is there a Chinese (or Japanese) proverb to the effect that "no one who rises before dawn 360 days a year
  will fail to make their family rich"? The GM heard it but has no source. Find it with its original text, or report it
  SILENT. (Whatever the answer, the toolkit will carry it only as a Rokugani saying.)

### 7. Why the record is thin
- **Question**: what do historians say about why women's unpaid household work in Tokugawa Japan and Ming/Qing China was
  so rarely recorded, and which kinds of source (diaries, household instructions, surveys, later ethnography) do they
  use to reconstruct it anyway? This is for an explicit note on the page.
- **Figure needed**: the historiographic statement, with its author, and the kinds of source it names.
