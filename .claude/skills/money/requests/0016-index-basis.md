**Money research: putting the goods-dollar koku on a sound index-number basis - like-for-like modern goods, era coverage, and which mean to take**

Questions from gm-assistant feature 223 (`specs/223-koku-index-reading/`), asked by the GM (2026-10-25). The GM's
words, from dictation ("cuckoo" = koku):

> if the amount of rice that one koku is meant to represent is about 150 kilograms [...] one way to perform the
> conversion of what a koku is worth is to simply ask, what does 150 kilograms of rice cost? [...] However, that is
> only one good. And different things have gotten cheaper at different rates between the pre-modern world and today.
> [...] if we are trying to compute the modern dollar amount that a koku is worth, then we would surely not add up all
> of these things together. We would be taking some kind of average, possibly a weighted average [...] we know that
> there were things that a household needed in a given year, and we know the approximate income in Koku of these
> pre-modern households. So I think it makes sense in that context to say that if we believe that a median pre-modern
> household had this basket of goods consumed over the course of a year, and then we looked up the modern prices for
> that exact basket of goods, then that would be another way to arrive at the modern equivalent [...] we are drawing
> from a number of different historical periods [...] we will probably have some kind of weighted average of the
> pre-modern prices that uses the different periods and how representative we think they are of our setting [...] can
> you take a step back and then think about what these baskets of goods are actually used to compute when they are
> invoked in academic papers

**Where things stand.** The goods-dollar koku is a Laspeyres index: the standard basket (Bassino & Ma 2005 Japan
basket A, plus salt, fuel and 3.31 m² of a back-tenement room) priced in Kyoto 1745-54 silver against a dated US
retail snapshot. That equals the 1745 spending-weighted mean of each good's own single-good koku (what a koku's silver
bought of that good, at today's dollars). Today (snapshot 2026-10, koku 151.5 kg of rice, 125.2 g of silver):

| good | share of 1745 spending | single-good koku | modern price used |
|---|---|---|---|
| rice | 40.6% | $191 | long-grain, 20 lb bag, $1.26/kg |
| soybeans | 18.5% | $988 | ORGANIC dried, 25 lb, $6.52/kg (no conventional retail pack found) |
| housing | 11.0% | $4,384 | US average asking rent, $269/m²-year |
| fuel | 10.7% | $227 | a cord of seasoned hardwood, $15 a million BTU |
| cloth | 7.0% | $36 | cotton muslin by area, $0.93/m of a 36 cm bolt |
| lamp oil, edible oil | 3.5%, 1.4% | $100 | canola, $2.50/L |
| buckwheat and millets | 2.3% | $1,281 | hulled millet, 50 lb, $3.46/kg |
| barley and wheat | 1.8% | $1,310 | hard red wheat berries, 25 lb, $4.41/kg |
| fish | 1.8% | $2,014 | wild cod fillets, $19.45/kg |
| salt | 0.5% | $1,127 | pickling salt, $1.39/kg |
| beans | 0.9% | $671 | BLS dried beans |

Weighted arithmetic mean $875; weighted geometric mean $370; unweighted mean $1,036. The spread (36 to 4,384) is
the problem: part of it is real (cloth industrialized, housing did not), part is likely a mismatch between what was
bought then and what we priced now (organic soybeans and specialty wheat berries against mass-market rice; cod
fillets against whatever fish a Kyoto laborer ate). Bassino & Ma recorded only rice and lamp oil in Kyoto 1745-54;
the other reference prices are their reconstructions (diagram 0249).

### 1. What exactly was bought: the historical good, its form and its market level

- **Question**: for each basket good in Kyoto/Osaka c. 1745 (and where it differs, the other eras), what form and
  quality was it (fish: fresh, salted, dried sardine/niboshi, which species; soybeans: whole dry beans or miso/tofu
  equivalent; barley and wheat: whole grain or flour; buckwheat and millets: grain or noodles; oils: rapeseed,
  sesame, fish oil; cloth: what weave and width), and were the prices the record cites wholesale (the Dojima floor,
  a merchant house's purchase book) or retail? If wholesale, what retail markup per good is attested?
- **Figure needed**: per good, the form/quality, wholesale or retail, and a markup where one is found.
- **Use**: the modern snapshot must price the same thing at the same market level, or the single-good koku compares
  unlike goods.

### 2. The modern like-for-like good, priced at the matching level

- **Question**: given 1, which modern US product best matches each good (e.g. niboshi or canned sardines rather than
  cod fillets; conventional dried soybeans or a bulk/commodity price; wheat flour rather than wheat berries), and
  what does it cost, at retail (cheapest plain household pack from a national retailer, about 2-25 lb, as the
  snapshot rule says) and, where the historical price is wholesale, at wholesale (USDA/commodity)?
- **Figure needed**: per good, the product, a price per the good's unit (kg, liter, m, million BTU, m²-year) with a
  link, at each level asked.
- **Canon beside it**: the 2026-10 snapshot above; it is never edited, a corrected one is a new snapshot.

### 3. Housing: the right modern comparator for a back-tenement room

- **Question**: the reference room is a guess borrowed from Edo 1804-30 (no Kyoto or Osaka rent found). Is there a
  Kyoto/Osaka rent for c. 1745 (or the nearest)? And which modern figure matches a laborer's tenement share: average
  US asking rent (today's), a low-income or rooming-house rent, a cost-to-build per m² (materials and labor, no land),
  or a world-poverty-line housing allowance? Housing is the single biggest swing in the reading ($4,384 vs rice $191).
- **Figure needed**: the historical rent with its room size; the modern candidates per m²-year with links and what each
  measures.

### 4. Era coverage beyond rice

- **Question**: of the 23 eras now held, 6 price rice alone and most price 2-5 goods. For which eras can soybeans,
  fish, cloth, salt, fuel and housing be priced, observed rather than reconstructed? Japan first (Edo/Osaka/Kyoto,
  1620-1882), China beside it. And per era, which of its goods are observed and which are reconstructed or borrowed?
- **Figure needed**: new silver prices per good and era in the format of 0286 (grams of pure silver per unit, label,
  source), and a per-era list of observed vs reconstructed goods.
- **Use**: Rokugan's price of each good will become a weighted mean across eras of the price in rice (kg of rice per
  unit, so debasements and silver's own value drop out); an era that prices only rice adds nothing to that.

### 5. Which mean economic historians take, and whether rice is a sound numeraire

- **Question**: when a historical sum is put into modern money with a basket and the relative prices have moved by
  two orders of magnitude, what do economic historians do: Laspeyres (base-period weights), Paasche, Fisher, a
  geometric (Jevons/Törnqvist) mean, or report a range? Which papers put an Allen-style basket into modern dollars
  (Allen 2017 AER "Absolute poverty: when necessity displaces desire"; Moatsos's basic-needs lines; MeasuringWorth's
  real price vs labor value), and how? Is expressing each era's prices in rice (rather than silver) an accepted way to
  compare across debasements, or does rice's own harvest volatility make it unsound over multi-year eras?
- **Figure needed**: the method each source uses, with citations; whether a range or one figure is the norm.
- **Use**: the default headline and its range on the toolkit's `/money/koku` page.

### 6. Modern quantities, for a Paasche or Fisher reading

- **Question**: what does a US adult consume a year of each basket good (rice, soybeans as food, wheat, other grains,
  dry beans, fish, cooking oil, cloth/textiles in m², salt, home heating/cooking fuel in million BTU, housing m² per
  person)? With these the basket can also be weighted by modern spending.
- **Figure needed**: per good, a per-adult annual quantity with source (USDA ERS food availability, EIA RECS, Census
  AHS).
