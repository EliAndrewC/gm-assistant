# Primary sources read directly

Load this file when: pricing a household, servants or a gentleman's budget, or adding rows from a source already in hand.

A public-domain primary source can be read and cited directly: download it to the scratchpad, quote it verbatim in the
row's `notes`, and point `source` at its public URL. The diagram repository still archives it and checks the extraction
(request it by issue, `research.md`), because the author is not a reliable checker of their own quotes.

## The Complete Servant (Samuel and Sarah Adams, London, 1825)

[Project Gutenberg #69487](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/69487) (plain text: `.../ebooks/69487.txt.utf-8`), from
Internet Archive scans; a second scan is in the Wellcome Collection. Late Regency, pre-industrial household practice, a
decade after Austen. Its servant wages are in the ledger (label `comparative`, tag `complete-servant`), converted at
the Austen sheet's 1 GBP = 145 sen, citing diagram's checked questions:
[0252](https://github.com/EliAndrewC/diagram/blob/main/research/questions/0252-what-did-a-regency-english-household-pay-its-servants.html) (wages by post),
[0253](https://github.com/EliAndrewC/diagram/blob/main/research/questions/0253-how-did-a-regency-english-household-divide-its-income.html) (the income division and servants by income),
[0254](https://github.com/EliAndrewC/diagram/blob/main/research/questions/0254-what-did-provisions-and-household-goods-cost-in-regency-england.html) (provisions: mostly SILENT; post-chaise hire, gold and silver, the book's price).
The wages are cash on top of board, lodging, livery and perquisites; the book gives no London-country split.

**How a large income is divided** (the book's rule, "in round numbers", for a gentleman's net income): one-third
household expenses and provisions; one-quarter servants and equipage (horses, carriages, liveries); one-quarter clothes,
children's education, medical, pocket money and entertaining; one-eighth rent, taxes and repairs; 4.5% reserve. Smaller
incomes: each parent costs 1/4 to 1/3 of the total and each child 1/10 to 1/12.

**Servants kept by income** (abridged from the book's list):

| Income a year | Servants |
|---|---|
| GBP 100 | a widow or single lady: one young maid at 5-10 guineas |
| GBP 150-200 | a couple: one maid, at 10-14 guineas |
| GBP 300-400 | with children: two maids; at 400, a cook, a house-maid and a nursery-maid or boy |
| GBP 500-750 | three women and one or two men (livery servant or groom), a gardener occasionally |
| GBP 1,000-1,500 | four women and three men (coachman, footman, stable and garden help) |
| GBP 2,000-3,000 | eight women and eight men, now with a butler and valet |
| GBP 4,000-5,000 | eleven women and thirteen men, with a house steward |

A country gentleman at GBP 16,000-18,000 net spent no more than GBP 7,000 and kept about two dozen named servants
(itemized in the book). Board wages while the family was away were 10s a week for women and 12s for men. The men got a
pot of ale a day, the women a pint, besides table beer.

**Using it for Rokugan**: this is a comparative source (diagram feature 391): never a Rokugani price or wage, never the
value of a koku. It is a yardstick players recognize, and the shape of a household at a given income (how many
servants, what share goes to the stable and the table) is a useful cross-check on a samurai household. The
pound-to-koku rate is the weak link (see the GM questions in `SKILL.md`).
