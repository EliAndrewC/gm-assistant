# Urban features: state markers, trades, wells, gate furniture, caste geography

*Part of the Mode B settlement docs. The index, the `meta()` knobs, the workflow and the validator contract live in [`../settlements.md`](../settlements.md).*

**Load this file when:** drawing a town OR a city - this is the vocabulary both tiers share: notice board, justice works, trade works, tanning yards, stable yards, wells, drum tower, gate furniture, status zoning.

---

- **Several merchant houses keep an attached storehouse (*kura*).** Because most Rokugani farmers are **tenants**, their (often absentee) landlords' rent-rice and bulk goods are stored in town - over and above the ordinary inventory storeroom a shop already has. So a noticeable **minority** of businesses run the classic narrow-front / deep-lot merchant compound with a fireproof *kura* behind the shopfront. Call `s.merchant_storehouses(count=6)` AFTER placing the businesses: it tucks a small kura behind several merchants (the **back**, opposite the street-facing awning), drawn as an annex like the farmhouse `shed`, so it needs no open ground in the maximally-packed quarter (there is none). `town_has_merchant_storehouses` requires **>= 3** - more than a token one or two, but still a minority.
- **Notice board (kosatsuba) - the state's standing law at the settlement's busiest point** (`town_has_kosatsuba` default-on for every town, walled or not; `kosatsuba_by_the_road` sites it within ~60 ft of a road/main street; `s.kosatsuba(x, y, rot)`; opt out with `meta(kosatsuba=False)` for a suppressed or backwater seat). Every Edo town AND village kept the official edict board - standing shogunal/domain law, porter and packhorse rate tables, currency regulations, ban edicts - and its siting was a TRAFFIC decision, not an administrative one: busy street corners, checkpoints, bridgeheads, the gate or center of the settlement, "in a prominent position alongside the main highway". Edo's principal board stood at Nihonbashi (the bridgehead where the five highways began), post towns' mid-shuku by the transport office - not at the government office. **The TWO boards are distinct institutions and coexist** (multiple boards per settlement were normal): the settlement kosatsuba posts the state's standing law where everyone passes, deliberately imposing (like the execution ground, it was designed to represent the strength of authority); the magistrate's manor-gate board (part of the Mode A compound program - buildings.md "Notice board") posts the bench's OUTPUT - verdicts, edicts of the court, bounties - for the people who come TO the court (litigants, petitioners, bounty hunters). Because the manor deliberately sits at the settlement EDGE (`manor_gate_faces_town`), defaulting the town board to the manor's gate would put it exactly where the fewest feet pass - the inversion the siting check exists to prevent. Placement: an unwalled road-town puts it on the highway frontage at a busy node of the core (Hoshizora: the SW arrival stretch by the theater/flophouse - the packed merchant frontage upstream has no verge, which is honest); a walled town on the main street just inside the front gate (Hirameki). Drawn at TRUE size (~12x5 ft roofed frame over the 7x3 ft board, the Mode A size) wherever true size is legible - at hamlet/town grain (1 ft/px) that is a 12x5 px glyph, and the label carries the read. **Cities port the institution UP, not away** (`city_has_kosatsuba` + `city_kosatsuba_per_gate`, same siting check with the limit in REAL feet - 60 ft is 20 px at the 3 ft/px city grain; GM 2026-07-24): a real city posted MANY boards - Edo kept the principal high-board at Nihonbashi plus subsidiary boards at its gates and bridge approaches - so a city **draws the SET and labels ONE** (GM decisions 2026-07-24, superseding the initial one-board-stands-for-the-set depiction): the **principal board at the central market node** PLUS a board on every main gate's approach corridor - the presence floor is gates + 1 (`city_has_kosatsuba`), and `city_kosatsuba_per_gate` holds each gate's board within ~800 real ft of its gate (the corridor, not the furnished throat). Only ONE board carries the label - **whichever board has room for it**, no particular one (the same one-label convention as the fire towers and gate markets); an UNLABELED board also fits the tight gate verges a labeled one cannot - Tango's busier south gate could not hold the label box but takes the bare board fine. **At the coarse tiers the glyph is a LOCATION MARKER** (GM call 2026-07-24, taking the escape this paragraph had documented as available): the true frame draws 6x2.5 px at village grain and 4x1.7 px at city grain, and that DID prove too small - on Nagahara, two of the three boards were invisible until the GM went hunting for the missing one, and the third read only because it carried the label; rotated upright, a 1.7 px sliver beside a ward gate reads as gate hardware, not as a feature. So `s.kosatsuba` floors the DRAWN glyph at `KOSATSUBA_MARKER_MIN_PX` (11 px) on its long axis with the 12:5 aspect preserved, which is the wells' doctrine exactly (SKILL.md "to scale"): the marker denotes the board's to-scale LOCATION with legible pixels that are not themselves claimed to be to scale. The floor NEVER shrinks a board, so hamlet/town maps are unchanged; only village and city grain lift. Calibration: 11 px is what the true frame already draws at 1 ft/px, the tier where the board has always read - and it lands the city marker just above the ~8 px wellhead, the smallest city glyph that reliably reads. The manifest keeps TRUE `w`/`h` (a size audit still reads real feet) and adds `vw`/`vh`, the drawn box - which is what the placement reservation and `_struct_rect`'s overlap tests use, since collisions are about drawn pixels. **Villages and hamlets carry the board too** (GM 2026-07-24; `village_has_kosatsuba` / `hamlet_has_kosatsuba`, same opt-out and siting - the siting routes include the LANE network at these tiers): the ofuregaki circulars reached the peasantry through exactly this board, posted where the settlement's one lane carries everyone - by the headman's frontage or the lane junction/entrance, since the headman (or in a hamlet the senior farmer answering to the village headman) received, copied, and relayed the circulars and was REQUIRED to be functionally literate for the job. One reader per settlement makes the board work - notices were also read aloud by officials - so peasant literacy is no objection even at a 50-inhabitant hamlet; the board is also the state's standing presence in a place that may see a magistrate's deputy twice a year. At these tiers the board is AUTO-SITED by `s.place_kosatsuba()` (called inside `roll_village()` for seed-rolled maps - it draws no RNG, so the seed stream is untouched - and as a one-liner in the hand gens): a deterministic probe over the manifest's own route fields (the validator's same-source doctrine) that scores verge spots for the most dwellings within ~260 px and hugs the verge. The lane corridor's no-build clearance is deliberately BYPASSED (it is a HOUSE setback - homesteads must not crowd the tread - and at village grain it is wider than the whole 60-ft siting band): the board stands off only the tread itself, which is exactly the historical posture - the board hugs the roadside, that being the point.
- **The justice works - a punishment ground INSIDE, an execution ground OUTSIDE** (feature 015; `s.place_punishment_spot()`, `s.execution_ground(cx, cy, rot)`, `s.boundary_marker(x, y)`; opt out with `meta(punishment_spot=False)` / `meta(execution_ground=False)`). These are **two institutions, not one feature drawn twice**, and they are sited by opposite logics - conflating them is the modeling error the whole design exists to avoid.
  - **The punishment ground** is the everyday face of the magistrate's authority: a cangue frame, a flogging post, and a kneeling stone on ~30x12 ft of tamped earth at the market or the magistracy frontage. Its governing variable is **foot traffic**, exactly like the notice board's, which is why it is placed by the same kind of probe (`place_punishment_spot` scores street verges by the dwellings within ~260px and hugs the verge) rather than by a hand-picked rect. It draws **no board of its own** - the crime rides on the cangue, as the historical inscription did - and it stays inside the rampart where there is one. Checks: `{scale}_has_punishment_spot`, `punishment_spot_in_the_core`, `punishment_spot_by_the_traffic` (~60 real ft of a street), `punishment_spot_only_at_a_seat_of_justice`.
  - **The execution ground** is bare, unbunded waste ground on the way out of town, past the boundary stone. Furniture: crucifixion post SOCKETS (posts were raised for a sentence and taken down - the socket is what stands), an iron burning stake, a sand beheading bed, a head-display stand with its crime board facing the road, a well for washing the blade, and a disposal pit. Sized by tier: ~60x60 ft at a county seat (unfenced, weedy - it must read DISUSED), ~100x60 ft at a provincial city (screened on three sides, road side open), ~150-250 x 50-80 ft at a capital. `rot` lays the ground's **road side (local -y)** toward the road, the same convention the tanning yard uses for its water side. Checks: `{scale}_has_execution_ground`, `execution_ground_outside_the_settlement`, `execution_ground_by_the_road`, `execution_ground_past_the_boundary_marker`, `execution_ground_clear_of_the_dead`, `execution_ground_off_the_farmland`, `execution_ground_on_the_outcast_side`, `execution_ground_only_at_a_seat_of_justice`.
  - **The boundary stone** (dosojin) is a LOCATION MARKER in the wells' sense - a real stone is ~3 ft, sub-glyph at every tier, so the true footprint is in `w`/`h` and the drawn box in `vw`/`vh`. It must stand **outside** the settlement, between it and the ground.
  - **Placement order:** the punishment ground goes in AFTER the urban packs and the notice board (it is probed onto a verge the engine will actually give it); the execution ground and its stone go in BESIDE THE FUNERARY CLUSTER, before the hinterland scrub and `village_grove`, so nothing is drawn over them. All three are `_CROP_HARD`/`_CROP_CITY` keys - they set the frame, because a ground clipped at the edge would read as "somewhere off that way", which is the one thing its siting is not.
  - **Practical traps, all paid for once already.** The ground must sit inside the map's existing view: `crop_to_content` clamps the frame to the canvas, and on a city `crop_city` will happily drag the frame out to reach a far-flung ground and thereby detach the road from the edge it must run off. A verge-hugging feature's default below-label lands on the frontage it hugs, so `place_punishment_spot` probes the label outward too - and the label test uses each building's **axis-aligned** box, which for a rotated shopfront is much larger than its `w`/`h`.
- **The FARM BELT is within reach of wells too** (`farm_wells_within_reach`, town+city; `s.farm_wells()` after `s.farmsteads()`; fixtures: Tango + Hirameki pre-fix). The urban idobata checks scope to commoner dwellings in the core, so the farmsteads ringing the outside fields had NO well coverage at all - Nagahara's interior steadings sat a median ~970 real ft from the nearest well (GM's eye: "too sparse", 2026-07-21). The rule: **no farmhouse stands more than 500 REAL FEET from a well** (a ~2-minute bucket walk; historically a farmstead often dug its own dooryard well, and Rokugan's unusually well-run domains sink wells liberally - the same liberty as the literal urban idobata count). **Map-edge steadings (within 150 real ft of the view edge) are exempt** - their fields already run off-map and their well is presumed just off the edge with the rest of the steading. The placer seats each well in a steading's dooryard or the open margin between steadings (the belt's ground is mostly crop, where a wellhead may not stand); where the only open ground is field-ENVELOPE rim slack, that is a legitimate seat (unplanted margin), tested against the DRAWN plots instead of the smoothed outline.
- **Public WELLS dot the commoner warren** (`city_neighborhoods_have_wells`, `city_wells_in_block_interiors`). Ensuring every commoner could draw water was a defining civic concern of a premodern city; the communal neighborhood well (井戸, the *idobata* social hub) served a courtyard / cluster of ~10-20 households, so the warren is sprinkled with them - one within a short walk of any home. They are the ONLY visible part of the water system: the aqueducts, underground cisterns, and rain barrels that fed the shafts are too small or literally subterranean and stay OFF the map. A wellhead (`s.well(x, y)`) is a stone curb under an open-sided roof, SMALLER than a house, and sits in a block INTERIOR off the lanes. Scatter them with **`s.place_wells(bbox, spacing)`** (a grid that tries a few offsets per cell, dropping any candidate on a lane / compound / the bound via the same `_fits` test the houses use) BEFORE that quarter's `s.pack(...)` so the houses flow around them; for a cramped, lane-laced quarter the grid can't reach (Tango's shallow burakumin lane-strip, the gap-fill band between the alleys), hand-seed a candidate list through **`s.well_at(x, y)`** (places only if the spot is clear). Tango runs ~47 wells (roughly the literal count for ~600 households at ~1 per 10-20, in the same draw-everything spirit as the houses). Two checks enforce this from different angles. **Coverage** (`city_neighborhoods_have_wells`): fires if any commoner dwelling (laborer/burakumin/merchant kinds) INSIDE the walls is more than ~290px from a well - samurai have private wells in their compounds (excluded), servants are interleaved and share the commoners' wells (ride along), and a transient gate market OUTSIDE the wall is not housing (excluded). **Density** (`city_well_density_sufficient`): assigning each commoner dwelling (servants included) to its NEAREST well, fires if any well is the nearest for more than **26** households - because coverage alone can look fine on AVERAGE while the busiest wells in the dense laborer warren are swamped (the 290px reach is satisfied by one well per radius, but a dense warren packs far more than 20 households into that radius). The ceiling is a touch above the historical 20 because the nearest-well split over-counts where two wells are nearly equidistant. The dense laborer/gap-fill quarters therefore need tighter `place_wells` spacing than the merchant quarter. `city_wells_in_block_interiors` is the placement backstop: a wellhead must sit clear of the lanes and not overlap a building/compound. **The SAMURAI/GOVERNMENT quarter has NO public wells** (`city_samurai_quarter_has_no_public_wells`): samurai drew from PRIVATE wells inside their own walled compounds, and gathering at the communal *idobata* was a commoner-district institution (beneath samurai status) - so a public wellhead must not sit among the samurai dwellings. The check is RELATIVE (a well is "in the samurai quarter" if most of its nearest dwellings are samurai), so it correctly ignores a commoner well sitting a block from the quarter across the ward fence rather than relying on a brittle absolute distance. Don't call `place_wells`/`well_at` in the samurai quarter. The drawn wellhead is a **location MARKER under the stroke convention** (GM ruling 2026-07-21, see SKILL.md "to scale": a real curb is ~3-4 ft, sub-glyph at every tier, so the marker denotes the well's to-scale location without claiming its own pixels are to scale) and **scales with the map grain**: `s.well` draws its ~24.8 ft marker footprint through `px()` (city ~8px, town ~25px, village ~12px) and records the drawn radius as `vr` on each well. A fixed-pixel wellhead looked right in a dense city but shrank to a quarter of a house at village/town scale, where buildings are drawn at full size - the well must track the same grain. `wells_sized_to_buildings` enforces this at EVERY scale: mean well diameter over the median dwelling's larger dimension must land in **[0.35, 0.85]** (a well is naturally smaller than a house, but not a dot). The reserved footprint and block-poly also scale with `vr`, so at every scale the well still reserves only a TIGHT courtyard (houses ring it closely) - each well costs roughly its own footprint, not a whole house-plot, so keep that reservation small or a city's worth of wells visibly deflates the population. NOTE wells still displace SOME housing, so re-check the population/caste bands after adding them (to keep Tango's depicted population a little above its declared 3,000 - dwellings x5 - the laborer packs are stepped tight, and the busiest west edge of the warren is given a few extra `well_at` wells so no well exceeds the density ceiling).
- **The RURAL tiers need wells too** (`settlement_has_wells`, `settlement_dwellings_watered`, for `town` / `village` / `hamlet`). Every settlement keeps communal wells - one per ~20-25 households - so `s.place_wells(<dwelling bbox>, spacing=..., near=85)` AFTER the farmhouses. **Pass `near=` for the rural tiers** (the city omits it): the bare grid would scatter wells out into the open fields and countryside between the field-rings, but `near=N` only keeps a candidate that has a dwelling within `N` px, so wells sit AMONG the houses. (A well must serve households around it; `wells_among_dwellings`, below, enforces this at every scale.) A dispersed farm household may instead draw from the **irrigation network it sits beside**, so a dwelling counts as *watered* if a well **OR** a channel / pond / stream / moat is within reach (~380px at the rural grain): the well count makes wells PRESENT, the coverage check makes sure no household is left dry. Tune the spacing per map (the hamlet shares ~1-2, a 60-household village ~6-9, a town ~16) so the worst-served dwelling - usually a far field-ring farmhouse - is within reach; the city tier keeps its own finer suite (density, block-interior placement, the samurai-quarter exemption) instead.
- **A well must sit AMONG the dwellings it serves** (`wells_among_dwellings`, every scale): a communal well is the draw-point for the households around it, so one out in open ground with no building within ~95px is unreal. A city's `s.pack(...)` runs after `place_wells` and fills in around the wells (so they end up surrounded); the rural tiers must use `place_wells(..., near=...)` (their grid runs after the houses and would otherwise drop wells into the fields). This is the bug it caught: Hirameki's wells scattered into the empty countryside.
- **A set-apart SHRINE keeps its OWN WELL close by** (`remote_shrine_has_own_well`, rural tiers). A shrine/temple set sufficiently apart from the village (its nearest dwelling is >150px away) is too far to use the village's shared wells, so it needs its OWN ablution well (*temizu*) right beside it - and specifically a WELL, not just a nearby ditch/pond (those are not ablution sources). A shrine among/near the houses shares theirs (exempt). Place it with **`s.shrine_well(cx, cy)`** AFTER the hall + houses + village wells: it tries widening rings until a well fits clear of the hall/torii/graveyard/lanes, so a large hall pushes its well onto an outer ring; the "close by" test is measured to the hall's EDGE (a big monastery's well sits further from its center), as is `wells_among_dwellings` (a shrine's own well stands beside the shrine, not among houses, so religious halls count as served). **A HILLTOP/mountain shrine is exempt** (it sits on `M['hill']`) - it draws from a spring/basin, and you cannot dig a well on a hill (no current pool map uses a hill-shrine; the exemption stands for any map that does). Both Hoshigaoka's water-mouth shrine and Kikuta's flat-ground Benten sit apart from the houses, so each keeps its own ablution well beside the hall.
- **Wells - research + deliberate liberty** (`wells_sized_to_population`, GM 2026-07-21). *What the research found:* a pre-modern south-China rice village of ~70 households ran **1-3 communal drinking wells total, 2 typical** - drinking water came mostly from SURFACE water (canal/pond/stream, stored in vats, settled, boiled), which is why schistosomiasis was endemic in the Yangtze rice belt for two millennia and why >70% of rural China still drank untreated surface water in the early 1980s; wells were expensive durable capital financed by per-family subscription and dug only as surface quality forced; the classical jingtian "8 families per well" is a Warring-States ideal nobody practiced. Japan corroborates from the other side: valley villages sited on springs/streams needed few or no communal wells, the famous dense pattern (~10-18 households per well, the idobata gossip circle) is URBAN nagaya tenement density, and per-household wells appear only on shallow-water-table plains. Capacity is never the constraint (one open well serves ~400 people by Sphere/UNICEF anchors, and a nucleated village is ~250m across so carrying distance never binds) - digging cost and morning queueing are. *The decision (a DELIBERATE LIBERTY):* Rokugan is deliberately unusually well-run, and generous water infrastructure expresses that - so **villages run ~1 communal well per 8-26 households** (2-3x the historical count) and **hamlets down to per-farmstead wells (2-20 hh/well)**, the shallow-water-table pattern made standard. This is a knowing departure from the historical 1-3-wells baseline, chosen over historical fidelity for the same reason as the Imperial roads: prosperity is part of the setting's thesis. Shrine (temizu) ablution wells are engine-tagged `shrine: true` and excluded from the count.
- **A BELL-AND-DRUM TOWER stands at the main street crossing of every WALLED seat** (`s.drum_tower(x, y)`, `walled_settlement_has_drum_tower`, town + city scope) - the timekeeping/curfew institution: morning bell, evening drum, the dusk gate-closing, the five night watches. Exactly one combined tower (the paired towers are capital grammar), on a corner of the central crossing within ~80px of two non-parallel road/street segments, ~36 ft at city tier / ~30 ft at town tier (footprint RE-VERIFIED 2026-07-24 after the GM's eye caught the 70 ft first draft oversized - see the Historical grounding entry), heavier-built than the skeletal fire towers (fire watch is a separate institution and does not satisfy this). An unwalled town is exempt - its time signal is the monastery's bell, implied. Full research record under "The bell-and-drum tower" in Historical grounding below.
- **TRADE WORKS - the trades whose premises outgrow the shop glyph are FIRST-CLASS features** (GM decision 2026-07-24, farriery added 2026-07-25; full research record under "TRADE WORKS" in the historical-grounding section below; fixtures: the pinned pre-trades Tango + Nagahara). Every provincial city keeps: a **BREWERY** (`s.brewery`, `city_has_brewery`) - sake/miso/soy, the town's largest commercial building (a 96 ft vat hall + shopfront + rice kura + its OWN private well; brewers site on good water - Tango's stands by the pond, beside the dyer; the vat circles are the hall's fermentation tanks, drawn diagrammatically INSIDE the hall footprint); a **DYE WORKS** (`s.dye_yard`, `city_has_dye_works`) whose drying/rinsing yard sits ON water (within ~40px of a stream/channel/canal/pond/moat - dyers need rinsing water, NOT bulk transport, so landlocked cities keep them too; a WORKING watercourse suffices and a full river is NOT required: 1-2 county-seat dyers' spent liquor is trivial dilution in even a stream's flow, and the real pollution problem the GM asked about - dye runoff fouling the farmers' water - is a dyer-DISTRICT phenomenon of the capital tier, whose dye street belongs downstream of the city's intakes; Tango's dyer fills its vats from the pond and rinses to the DRAINAGE side, the channels that run out to the moat, never into the field-supply channels); an **OIL PRESS** (`s.oil_press`, `city_has_oil_press`) - a barn-scale wedge-and-beam works with an ox-driven mill ring, toward the edge; a **PAWNSHOP** (`s.pawnshop`, `city_has_pawnshop`) - an ordinary shopfront whose tell is the 2-kura walled pledge court; a **BATHHOUSE** (`s.bathhouse`, `city_has_bathhouse`) - shophouse-scale + furnace chimney + a fenced FUEL YARD of firewood stacks behind, the furnace's wood store and the sento's one outsized appetite (China-first: commercial baths attested from the Song). the COUNT ROLLS FROM POPULATION (GM formula 2026-07-24, second refinement; `s.bathhouses(seats)` + the extended `city_has_bathhouse`): **1 bathhouse per full 2,000 population, plus a chance of ONE extra equal to the remainder fraction** - a 2,500 seat keeps 1 guaranteed + a 25% roll, a 3,000 seat 1 + 50%, a 4,000 seat exactly 2 (floored at 1; dedicated Random on the map seed - under this formula Tango rolls 1 and Nagahara 2). The 2,000 divisor is Edo's own peak ratio - 523 sento for ~1.1 million residents in 1808, about 1 per 2,100; the recorded roll (meta bathhouse_roll) must match the drawn count, so a stale hand count cannot ship; and a **KILN strictly OUTSIDE the walls** (`s.kiln`, `city_kiln_outside_walls` - fire law + smoke). The other banished nuisance trade, the **TANNING YARD**, is now drawn too - and it is the one trade work that is NOT city-only, so its rules live under "TANNING YARDS" in the historical-grounding section below. A **RIVER-PORT city** (`meta(river_port=True)`, Nagahara) also keeps a riverside **LUMBER YARD** (`s.lumber_yard`, `city_river_port_has_lumber_yard`, within ~60px of the bank) - timber moves by water at scale, so a landlocked city (Tango) has NONE: that asymmetry is the GM's deliberate Tango/Nagahara split The yard ABUTS the bank but never OVERLAPS the water (`lumber_yard_clear_of_water`, GM rule 2026-07-24): stacked timber stands on DRY ground - logs in the current float away, and the landing is the jetty's job. Tested at each watercourse's REAL half-width, because the generic no_structure_on_stream check's fixed ~6px half-width is tuned for village brooks and cannot see a corner swallowed by a 40px river (the pinned Nagahara fixture is exactly that capture). Every city also keeps a **FARRIER** (`s.farrier`, `city_has_farrier`) - an open-sided forge shed (hearth, smoke hood, anvil, quench tub) plus the working APRON in front, carrying the shoeing post and the OX-SHOEING FRAME, the timber stocks a cloven-hoofed ox must be slung in because it cannot balance on three legs. It stands ON the gate caravan yard beside the stables (`farrier_serves_a_stables`, within ~250 real ft) and NEVER abutting the stall range (`farrier_keeps_fire_gap`, >= ~6 real ft clear of every footprint - an open forge against hay and timber is the fire a yard does not survive). At TOWN scale the farrier is conditional, not universal: only a town that declares `meta(imperial_road=True)` draws one (`imperial_road_town_has_farrier`) - a relay/post town works courier and caravan horses hard enough to keep a forge, an ordinary market town does not. That is the deliberate **Hoshizora/Hirameki split**: Hoshizora is a post town on the Imperial Road and keeps a farrier at its caravan stables; Hirameki, a walled market town off the road, draws none even though it has a caravan inn and stables of its own. Hamlets and villages never keep one. Rokugan shoes horses in IRON rather than straw - see the FARRIERY sub-entry under "TRADE WORKS" in the historical-grounding section for why, and for why that still leaves the ordinary town smith inside the shop rows. The NULL results are load-bearing too: the long tail of trades (tofu, teahouse, apothecary, cooper, carpenter, rice dealer, and the ordinary SMITH - whose hoof work changes his repertoire, not his footprint - plus the family hatago of the lodging program) deliberately stays inside the generic shop rows. The brewery's courtyard well records `private=True` and is excluded from ALL public-idobata accounting (reach, density, block-interior siting, the samurai-ward ban). Each trade record reserves its footprint AND its caption band (at the caption's width - wider than the building for short glyphs like the bathhouse), and the trade features + drum tower are in the labels-check victim set, so a foreign caption over a press is caught like one over a house. Budget: a "brewery compound" line + a combined "trade works" line in `citybudget.py`; the kiln and lumber yard sit outside the walls and cost no interior ground.
- **THE JUSTICE WORKS: WHY A COUNTY SEAT EXECUTES, AND WHY THE GROUND IS OUTSIDE** (drives the punishment-ground and execution-ground vocabulary above and their twelve checks; feature 015, 2026-07-25).
  - *The China/Japan split, and how it resolves.* This is one of the few places the two grounding traditions genuinely disagree, so the reconciliation is load-bearing rather than decorative. **Japan** pushed executions OUT of the settlement for kegare (death pollution) - Edo's three grounds all sat at highway entrances outside the center, castle towns monopolized them, and village authority topped out at banishment. **China** executed in the marketplace (棄市 *qishi*, "abandon in the market"; Qing Beijing's Caishikou was the vegetable market at a crossroads outside Xuanwumen), and although a county magistrate could not CONFIRM a death sentence - capital cases climbed to the Board of Punishments and the emperor's autumn check-marking (秋審 *qiushen*) - the confirmed sentence came back DOWN and was carried out at the county seat where the crime happened, because local deterrence was the point. So: **China supplies the jurisdiction (the seat executes), Japan supplies the siting (past the built edge).** That is exactly what this file's town doctrine already asks for - "what would a Chinese county seat administered by samurai do?"
  - *It explains a canon line we had never used.* `budgets.md` funds "prisoner food, jail upkeep, court materials" at county level - a jail with **no execution line** - while "ceremonial executions" appear only in the domain and Imperial budgets. Read through the Chinese chain that is precisely right: the county jail is a **holding pen for the condemned while the warrant travels**, not a punishment in itself. (`SKILL.md` already records the same shape for Mode A: Edo detention was a holding function, not imprisonment-as-punishment.)
  - *The governing variables.* For the execution ground: **the road and the direction pollution runs** - NOT population and not the settlement's geometric edge. The ground exists to be seen by travelers arriving, so it hugs the busiest way out; kegare puts it downwind, downstream, and on the outcast side, which is why `execution_ground_on_the_outcast_side` measures against the burakumin quarter (per `l7r.md`, burakumin perform every execution that is not a samurai's). For the punishment ground: **foot traffic**. A generator usually gets the existence of a thing right and its governing variable wrong; these two are the ones to protect.
  - *Where the sizes come from.* The hard anchor is **Suzugamori: 74 x 16.2 m (~243 x 53 ft, about a third of an acre), serving Edo - a city of one million - for 220 years.** Execution grounds are SMALL; the deterrent is the sight of the posts from the highway, not acreage. The tier ladder scales down from that by execution VOLUME, which is also what sets each tier's character: at ~1-3 executions per 100,000 per year, a county of ~7,000-8,000 inhabitants reaches the formal channel about **once every 5-10 years** (bandit sweeps add batches), a province sees on the order of one a year, a domain capital a handful. Hence the county ground is drawn weedy with EMPTY post sockets and no hoarding: a busy scaffold at county scale would assert something false about how often the Empire kills.
  - *The beating is not here.* The design originally put flogging at the street installation; the Chinese evidence moved it. The bamboo (笞 / 杖) was a COURT act administered inside the yamen courtyard before the magistrate's bench - which in our maps is the Mode A magistracy, unchanged by this feature - while the 枷 *jia* (cangue) was the public part, worn one to six months with the offender's name, crime, and sentence inscribed on the boards for passers-by to read. So the street feature DISPLAYS; it does not try. It also draws no notice board, because the crime text rides on the cangue and the settlement already has a kosatsuba.
  - *A deliberate departure from literal reality, disclosed.* `EXECUTION_GROUND_DEAD_CLEAR_FT = 400` is a **map-legibility floor, not a historical measurement**. In reality the executed went into a pit AT the ground (Kozukappara's burials were haphazard enough that a memorial hall was founded beside it in 1667 for that reason) while the community's dead went to temple graveyards **elsewhere in the city entirely** - usually a different road out of town, often a mile off. Our maps are a few thousand feet across and cannot hold that, so the separation is compressed and the RELATIVE ordering kept honest instead: the execution ground is always the further-out, more polluted of the two. The number was first set at 150 ft by analogy to the project's existing pollution constant (the cremation ground and tanning yard demand 120 ft clear of dwellings, and two POLLUTED grounds need more separation than polluted-from-clean does). The **Principle XII artifact review raised it to 400** - and that is the clearest example on record of why that gate exists: Nagahara's ground passed the 150 ft check at 225 ft and still read, in the rendered PNG, as part of the burial cluster beside it. 400 ft is derived from the picture rather than by analogy - at the coarsest grain we draw (3 ft/px, so 133 px) it is the distance at which the two are unmistakably two places.
  - *Two rules that had to be loosened, and why that was right rather than convenient.* (1) `execution_ground_by_the_road` measures ~120 real ft to a drawn ROAD but ~400 ft to a GATE: a road is a line the ground can hug (Suzugamori sat on the Tokaido), while a gate is a point standing in for the road that leaves it, and Edo's grounds were miles out along their highways. Measuring a gate at the road's tolerance made the rule unsatisfiable on Hirameki, which draws no extramural road at all. (2) `execution_ground_on_the_outcast_side` tests DIRECTION only. It began as "beyond the quarter as well", which was unsatisfiable on a walled town whose extramural outcast quarter already sits at the canvas corner - and radius is an artifact of where the wall happens to be, while the claim the research actually supports (downwind, downstream, the outcast side) is directional.
  - *The boundary stone earns its place.* 道祖神 *dosojin* / *sae no kami* stand at village boundaries, passes, and crossroads, and the etymology is the point: *sae* means "to block", and the deity's job is to stop evil, pestilence, and pollution from ENTERING. That converts "outside the settlement" from a vague spatial claim into a stated ritual boundary - the ground is not merely far from the houses, it is on the far side of the stone that keeps pollution out - which is why the check also demands the stone stand outside the wall. A dosojin inside the rampart bounds nothing.

- **TRADE WORKS - which trades outgrow the generic shop glyph, and why** (drives `s.brewery`, `s.dye_yard`, `s.lumber_yard`, `s.oil_press`, `s.pawnshop`, `s.bathhouse`/`s.bathhouses`, `s.kiln`, `s.farrier` and their checks; the DRAWN rules live under "Provincial cities" above. Research pass 2026-07-24, from the GM's question during the provincial-city audit - "are there trades whose real premises, with their attached works, yards, and outbuildings, are materially bigger than a shophouse, i.e. significant space our maps are not representing?" - plus the farriery reversal of 2026-07-25.)
  - *Where this entry came from.* It absorbs the former `trade-footprint-research.md`, a separate per-question research file. That file had already duplicated its own conclusions into the rules paragraph above, and the duplication is exactly what bit: when the GM reversed the horseshoe finding, the stale claim "there are NO FARRIERS" was living in THREE places (the research file, the rules paragraph, and a `settlement.py` comment) and every one had to be found and fixed. The standing convention that follows: **the rule and its why belong in this file, stated once**; a standalone research file is for a derivation too long to inline (`flophouse-research.md`) or a dated point-in-time audit (`town-deep-audit.md`), and it must never restate a rule the operative doc also states.
  - *The frame.* Song/Ming China first, Edo Japan second. City maps at 1 px = 3 ft already draw commerce as generic ~48x32 ft shop rowhouses plus merchant kura and dedicated inn/stables/flophouse glyphs, so the only question is which trades BREAK that glyph.
  - *The shortlist - premises visibly bigger than a rowhouse at this scale.* **Sake/miso/soy brewery** (*sakagura*; Chinese *zaofang*) is the big one: even a small county-seat brewer needed a vat hall of 5-6 ft fermentation tanks, a koji room, a rice-steaming area with masonry kamado and chimney, its own well, and a rice kura - a minimal hall runs 60-120 ft x 30-40 ft BEHIND a normal shopfront, 3-8x the shophouse, and surviving small-town compounds reach whole-block scale (one Nagano brewer at ~1,500 tsubo / ~53,000 sq ft). It was very often the largest commercial building in a small town; expect 1-2 per seat of ~3,000, sited in town on good well water because brewers were town elite. **Dyer** (*kon-ya*): modest building, large GROUND signature - the workshop's sunken indigo vats and boiling hearth fit a deep shophouse, but the DRYING does not (poles, racks, and rooftop platforms dominate the block in Hiroshige's Kanda Konya-cho print) and rinsing happened in open water (*nunosarashi*), so a town dyer needs a ~2,000-5,000 sq ft drying yard (bolts run 35-40 ft) or riverbank frontage. **Lumber dealer** (*zaimokuya*): stacked timber and floated logs need a ~3,000-8,000 sq ft open yard with a river landing, at the edge of town near the downstream gate or wharf. **Oil presser** (*aburaya* / *youfang*): the wedge-and-beam press is a massive timber machine (beam 15-30 ft) plus a seed-roasting stove and an ox-driven edge-runner mill on a ~20-25 ft circular track - a fire-conscious press barn ~40-60 x 25-30 ft toward the edge. **Pawnshop** (*shichiya*): an ordinary shop whose tell is STORAGE - pledges are bulky, so a prosperous broker kept 2-3 fireproof kura in a walled rear court, i.e. the existing kura glyph multiplied rather than a new building type. **Bathhouse** (*sento*): an ordinary streetfront building with a rear furnace and chimney, whose visible extra is a ~1,000-2,000 sq ft firewood stack yard; commercial baths are China-first correct, attested from the Song, so a city may keep 1-2.
  - *The null results, which are equally load-bearing.* The long tail genuinely fits the shop glyph - tofu maker, noodle and confection shops, apothecary, teahouse, cooper, tatami maker, carpenter's shop (his big work happens on site), rice dealer (treadle-pestle hulling in-shop, grain in a rear kura). And some trades belong OUTSIDE town entirely: tile, pottery, charcoal, and lime kilns are pushed to the periphery or to nearby villages by fire law and smoke, and tanneries and slaughter go downstream-riverside with the extramural burakumin quarter, never inside.
  - *FARRIERY - the null result that was REVERSED (GM 2026-07-25).* The 2026-07-24 pass recorded a flat "NO FARRIERS", on the correct observation that Edo Japan did not shoe horses: it used woven straw horse-sandals (*umagutsu* / *umazouri*), and nailed iron shoeing arrived with Meiji. That is right about **Japan** and wrong about **this Rokugan**, for two reasons the GM's own notes already carried. **(1) Iron.** Japan's constraint was material, not ignorance - iron sand (*satetsu*) smelted with charcoal is a low-yield, fuel-hungry process, so iron went to blades, armor, and tools long before hooves. Rokugan is continental, with real ore bodies and whole iron districts already in `l7r.md` (Tatarano, "bellows plain"; Kuroiwa; Ubame's charcoal-sited furnaces; the Reiji hill works; Crab iron for the Wall). Ore is abundant and iron is a normal industrial good, while good STEEL stays scarce and expensive - which buys horseshoes, plain low-carbon iron, without deflating a good blade. **(2) Institutional mileage, more than paving.** Hard surfaces do abrade hoof, but the sharper driver is annual miles per horse: `budgets.md` gives ~5,000 waystations on ~50,000 miles of Imperial road at ~10-mile spacing - about 3x the density of the Tang *yi* network - plus 500-1,000 mounted couriers and mounted anti-banditry patrols. A courier horse working a fixed stage all year wears hoof at a rate no farm horse approaches, and straw sandals are consumables that wear out within a day's travel. Straw scales for a farmer walking an ox to the next village; it does not scale for institutionalized relay. **Canon already assumed this**: `budgets.md` staffs busy trunk-route waystations with "a smith for shoeing horses and weapon maintenance," and `l7r.md`'s Moto Khuyag is introduced as a **farrier** who offers to *reshoe* the PCs' horses - a Rokugani-born one, who learned the trade at Kyuden Shinjo. So this was a reconciliation, not a retcon; the diagram skill's null result was the thing out of step. Two arguments were considered and deliberately NOT relied on, because they do not survive checking: "later Chinese dynasties nailed iron shoes" (Chinese hoof practice ran mostly to trimming and strap-on or woven boots; nailed shoes are firmly a European development, and Rome's *cursus publicus* used strap-on **hipposandals**, not nails), and "the Mongol Yam proves it" (steppe horses on grass were famously ridden UNSHOD, which is a counterexample). The conclusion stands on iron price and relay mileage alone.
  - *What that changes on the map, and what it does not.* The SHAPE conclusion of the 2026-07-24 pass survives its own premise: even in Europe a farrier was a one-forge smithy with a bit of yard, and the big "farrier-forge-with-stable-yard" compound is a coaching-inn image that does not earn its footprint at 1 px = 3 ft. So an ordinary town smith (*kaji-ya*) who also shoes still draws as a generic shop rowhouse - shoeing changes his REPERTOIRE, not his premises - and no new establishment joins the shop rows. What earns real ground is only the place horses CONCENTRATE: the gate caravan yard and the Imperial-road relay town, where `s.farrier` draws a dedicated shed and apron. Two rules fall out of the physical facts. **It is not attached to the stables**: an open forge against a hay-and-timber stall range is a fire the yard does not survive, so real yards kept the smithy across the ground (`farrier_keeps_fire_gap`, anchored on `buildings.md`'s ~6-8 ft wooden-service fire gap; the measure runs from the whole recorded footprint, shed plus apron, which is deliberately conservative since the shed sits at the apron's far end). And **the apron carries an ox-shoeing frame**: Rokugan's draft animal is mostly the ox, a cloven hoof takes two plates per foot, and an ox cannot balance on three legs, so it must be slung in timber stocks - about 7 x 4 ft, drawn true-size like everything else. Sizes are the historical anchors with no legibility inflation: an 18-20 ft village smithy for the shed, and an apron a horse's length (~8 ft) plus room to lead one in and turn it. **Straw survives** everywhere iron is not worth it - peasant pack horses, oxen on soft paddy tracks, poor and mountain provinces - because it is free, not because iron is unknown; that is setting texture rather than a drawn feature, and it lives in `budgets.md`.
  - *Frequency by scale, and the reasoning behind it.* Rokugan's horse population is LOW relative to Europe - peasants work oxen, and horses are a samurai and Imperial animal - so farriery is not a mass trade and the count must not scale with population the way the bathhouse does. Provincial city: exactly one, at a gate caravan yard (not one per gate). Town: only on an Imperial road, which is why `meta(imperial_road=True)` gates the check rather than town scale alone - at ~10-mile waystation spacing plenty of towns simply are not on the network, and that non-uniformity is the point. Village and hamlet: never; the village smith trims hooves and tacks on straw with no dedicated premises. A county magistracy (Mode A) keeps only 2-4 horses per `buildings.md`, so it keeps no permanent forge either - the smith walks over from town.
  - *The implemented features* (GM 2026-07-24, "I'd like all of them"): brewery compound (`city_has_brewery`, vat hall + shopfront + rice kura + its own PRIVATE well; both cities keep one, and Tango's stands on the pond); dyer's drying yard (`city_has_dye_works`, must sit ON water - both cities, since dyeing needs rinsing water rather than bulk transport, so a landlocked city keeps its dyers; Tango's rinses to the DRAINAGE side, never the field-supply channels); riverside lumber yard (`city_river_port_has_lumber_yard`, gated on `meta(river_port=True)` - Nagahara only, since timber is the one trade that genuinely needs water transport at scale, and the yard abuts the bank but never overlaps the water); oil-press barn (`city_has_oil_press`); pawnshop (`city_has_pawnshop`); bathhouse (`city_has_bathhouse`, count rolled from population); kiln (`city_kiln_outside_walls`); farrier (`city_has_farrier` / `imperial_road_town_has_farrier`, 2026-07-25). The tanning yard is drawn too and has its own entry below.

- **TANNING YARDS - the burakumin trade, and the feature that decides where their quarter sits** (drives `s.tanning_yard` and the checks `settlement_has_tanning_yard`, `tanning_yard_on_water`, `tanning_yard_outside_walls`, `tanning_yard_clear_of_dwellings`, `tanning_yard_clear_of_water`, `tanning_yard_clear_of_fields`, `tanning_yard_discharges_to_nothing_drawn_from`, `tanning_yard_below_every_intake`; GM 2026-07-24, overlap + downstream rules 2026-07-25. Fixtures in `pool/regressions/`, including `settlement_has_tanning_yard_fires_on_the_pre_tannery_hirameki.json` - the real walled town as it stood before this feature existed - plus the real Tango-yard-in-the-stream and Hoshizora-yard-on-the-paddy captures.)
  - *Why towns tan too, and this is not a city-only trade.* The GM's opening guess was that most towns would have no tannery. The research says otherwise, because of where hides come from: NOT butchery but **fallen draft stock**. Under the Tokugawa status order the kawata held carcass rights over a defined service territory (*danna-ba*) - when an ox or horse died it became that community's property automatically, with no compensation to the owner. So a county town's ~12 burakumin households (budgets.md town tier: 60 inhabitants) work the whole county's dead animals, not just the town's. L7R canon supplies the same stream from the other end: `l7r.md` has daimyo giving surplus horses to village headmen to distribute to farms as draft animals, and those horses "often slaughtered and eaten when the need for meat outweighs the capacity of the peasants to feed those horses."
  - *Throughput is small, and scales with the territory served - which is what `pits` encodes.* A county of ~6,800 inhabitants sheds on the order of a couple dozen carcasses a year, so the town yard is a **seasonal works of ~4 pits**, never an industry. A provincial city adds the daimyo's stable (several hundred horses per domain) and the armor/saddle/drum/bellows demand of its ~300 samurai, so it runs **~12**. That ratio is why the city yard draws roughly 1.7x the town yard's area rather than 5x: pits, not prestige, set the size. For comparison, the largest excavated urban tannery at Northampton ran 36-37 pits - far past anything in this pool.
  - *WATER is the real gate, not settlement size.* Tanning is a water process: the Japanese *shironameshi* method stakes raw hides in the river for one week in summer and two in winter before the hair is knifed off, and every archaeologically-attested tannery sits on a watercourse at the settlement's edge. The caste's own name for itself was *kawaramono*, "riverbed people." Hence the check's shape: a town or city with a burakumin quarter **and** a watercourse must have a yard; one with no running water keeps none whatever its size, and is exempt rather than failing.
  - *Placement rules.* The yard stands (a) ON the water - its ground must abut the bank within ~20 ft, because a yard set back on dry ground could not work; (b) on DRY ground even so - the footprint stops AT the bank and only the staking frames reach into the shallows; (c) strictly OUTSIDE the walls, with the kiln - the workers may live in-wall, the work may not; (d) DOWNSTREAM of everything the settlement draws from; and (e) NEVER on cropland. (d) WAS a judgment call for want of flow data; since the water-flow work it is two real checks. `tanning_yard_discharges_to_nothing_drawn_from`: the yard's own course must end off-map (or in a field drain that does), never in a pond, a field, an irrigation ditch or the moat - and the sink is read by FLOW, not by polyline order, since `frm`/`to` are anchored positionally. `tanning_yard_below_every_intake`: no sluice, weir or channel head on that same course may lie below the yard, compared by ARC POSITION along the course. Graph topology alone cannot see the second one - a channel tapping the river 200 ft below the yard and one tapping it 200 ft above are the same edge - which is why the check measures distance along the water rather than walking the anchor graph.
  - *(b) is enforced by its own check, `tanning_yard_clear_of_water` (GM 2026-07-25), not by the generic `no_structure_on_stream`* - which demonstrably cannot see it: the generic check tests a fixed ~6 px half-width tuned for village brooks (the same gap `lumber_yard_clear_of_water` was built for), and it let the real Tango yard sit ~10 ft over its 22 px stream and the real Hoshizora yard sit ON a drain ditch (both frozen in `pool/regressions/`). The dedicated check tests the yard rect - with its true rotation - against every watercourse's REAL half-width (streams, channels, canals, river, moat, field ditches, pond) via segment-to-rect distance, so a thin ditch THREADING UNDER the rect between its corners is caught too, which corner-sampling misses. The why: the soaking pits are dug earth (a pit dug below the waterline is just more stream), and the racks cure hides for 2-4 months - ground that floods rots the stock. The staking frames are the ONE deliberate in-water element, drawn BEYOND the ground rect out in the shallows - so a yard whose GROUND reads as "a platform over the water" is always this defect, never a design; there is no platform variant. Exact abutment of the bank line is legal, and the good maps hug it (Nagahara clears its bank by ~1 ft).
  - *(e) is `tanning_yard_clear_of_fields` (GM 2026-07-25): the yard never overlaps a field outline, dry plot, or flower field.* The why is the trade's whole siting logic: kawaramono - "riverbed people" - worked the unplowable floodway margins precisely because taxed, producing land was never theirs to take, so a yard drawn on a field asserts ground that is simultaneously cropped and ruined for cropping. Physically a paddy is a flooded basin that cannot carry a tamped work floor, and the pits' lime and bate liquor poison the soil. Abutting a field is fine - marginal bank ground borders cultivation everywhere - only OVERLAP fires.
  - *WHY tannery effluent must not reach a paddy intake - and which part of it actually does the harm* (GM question 2026-07-25). Tanning in general is famous for urine and dung: hides were limed OR soaked in stale urine to loosen the hair, then "bated" by kneading them in dog or pigeon dung. But the method these maps commit to is Japanese **shironameshi**, which uses none of it - no lime, no tannin, no dung, no urine. The hides soak in the river for 1-2 weeks (river bacteria do the unhairing), the hair is knifed off, and the hide is finished with **salt and rapeseed oil** alone. So the discharge is three things: two weeks of controlled putrefaction, SALT, and an oil film. **The salt is what a rice paddy cannot take.** Rice is the most salt-sensitive cereal, and it is more sensitive to the salinity of the FIELD WATER than of the soil - a yield threshold around 0.9 dS/m - so brine arriving down an irrigation ditch into a flooded paddy is the precise wrong input. Note the counterintuitive corner, so nobody "improves" the rule later by reasoning from rot: the ORGANIC half of the effluent is arguably fertilizer (Rokugani farmers deliberately spread night soil), so putrefaction is the WEAK argument here. The rule stands on salt, the oil film, and the stench.

  - *Who it may stand next to - and the exemption that is deliberate.* The stench separation is the crematory's existing **120 ft** (`town_has_cremation_ground`), reused rather than freshly invented, since that is already this project's figure for "a nuisance kept off the houses." **Burakumin dwellings are exempt from it.** That is not an oversight: kawaramono lived on the ground they worked, and that adjacency is precisely what the segregated quarter IS. Counting their houses as something to stay away from inverts the whole institution - and it did, in the first pass here, which returned zero viable sites at Hoshizora until the exemption was added.
  - *A long walk to work is not a defect* (GM ruling 2026-07-24). Tango's quarter is in-wall NW and its yard is outside the SE rim; Hoshizora's crematory is across town from its quarter. Do not "fix" these by moving quarters closer to their work: being made to walk is one of the indignities the caste is subject to even in a Rokugan that is kinder to its underclasses than the historical societies it draws on. There is deliberately NO proximity check pairing a yard with its quarter.
  - *Per-map siting, and why each is where it is.* **Nagahara** is the textbook case - west bank of the Hayakawa, south of the wall, below the moat's outflow junction (y~1521), below the wharf and below the in-wall canal's dyer; same bank as the SE quarter, so nobody crosses water, and the funerary complex keeps the far bank. **Hirameki** is nearly free: its burakumin were already outside the wall at the south (downhill) end of the quarter on the east valley stream, so the yard takes the strip between quarter and bank. **Tango** is the instructive one. Its outfall is NOT a waste channel - it is an irrigation supply, with fs3 tapping it at (1936,1880) and fse1 at (1995,2020) (GM 2026-07-23) - so the obvious seat below the samurai estates sits ABOVE fse1's intake and fouls the SE paddies. `tanning_yard_below_every_intake` caught exactly that on its first run, against an intake 74 px BELOW the visible frame (fse1's field was cropped out entirely), which is why no amount of looking at the render would have found it. The yard therefore goes below BOTH taps and clear of fse1's feeder corridor, which pulls the crop ~180 px south. That is a FEATURE, not a cost: the added strip carries the SE farmland, the outfall and the Imperial road, and the map reads better balanced for it. An intermediate conclusion that Tango simply could not host a tannery was WRONG - the first attempt at a downstream seat was placed too far south (y2120), which pushed the frame past the off-map tails of the Imperial road and a field drain and made the cascade look unavoidable. A shallower seat (y2068) needs only the road and outfall tails extended, with the field drain still inside the 32 px offmap tolerance. Lesson: when a feature drags the frame, try the SHALLOWEST legal seat before concluding the map cannot hold it. (`meta(tannery=False)` remains as the general opt-out for a settlement that genuinely has water but no site on it - it is just not Tango.) **Hoshizora** has no watercourse near its quarter at all (the valley stream runs across the far west), so the yard takes the drainage ditch under the NE comb, ~280 ft below the quarter's doors - defensible rather than merely convenient, because that comb is fed by its own brook off the high ground NE and discharges OFF-MAP EAST, hydrologically separate from the west stream the town drinks and irrigates from. Hoshizora is also the one map drawn with `water="ditch"`: an irrigation drain has no current to stake hides in, so that yard ponds its own soak behind a gated intake cut instead of showing staking frames.

- **The bell-and-drum tower - one per WALLED seat, at the main crossing** (drives `s.drum_tower`, `walled_settlement_has_drum_tower`, the citybudget "bell-and-drum tower" line; GM decision 2026-07-24; fixtures: pre-tower Tango + pre-tower Hirameki). *What the research found:* the "morning bell, evening drum" institution (dawn gate-opening, the dusk gate-closing that began the street curfew, the five night watches, alarm and ceremony) was part of the standard Ming county-seat kit alongside the yamen, the Confucian temple, and the City God temple - common approaching standard in walled northern seats. A county seat had ONE tower - a drum tower, a combined bell-and-drum tower, or a market tower straddling the main street; the paired gulou + zhonglou on an axis is CAPITAL grammar (Beijing, Xi'an), and Pingyao, a wealthy county seat, has exactly one (the ~60 ft Market Tower on South Street). County-tier form (footprint RE-VERIFIED 2026-07-24, second research pass after the GM's eye caught the first-draft 70 ft platform oversized): **Pingyao's Market Tower - the wealthy-county showpiece - is ATTESTED at 133.4 m^2 plan, ~38 ft square**; these towers dominated by HEIGHT (50-60 ft), not plan, and Kawagoe's famous time-bell tower served a town of tens of thousands from a <20 ft base. The 50-66 ft square examples (Dingbian 52 ft, Xingcheng 66 ft) are the tower-over-street-arch type - garrison/prefecture fabric, never a 3,000-person seat - and the original 60-80 ft figure was contaminated by them. The drawn band is therefore **30-45 ft: ~36 ft at city tier, ~30 ft at town tier** - one rowhouse width, which is exactly how they read in plan. Siting: the central crossroads near (not inside) the yamen; poorer seats made do with a *qiaolou* - a drum tower over the yamen's own gate (a recognized cheaper variant, not drawn here). Critically, FIRE WATCH WAS A SEPARATE INSTITUTION in both reference cultures - Song Kaifeng ran 20+ dedicated *wanghuolou* fire-lookout towers with a standing brigade, and Edo split the licensed *toki no kane* time bell from the *hinomi-yagura* fire towers - so the fire towers do not cover the timekeeping/curfew niche. *The decision it drove:* every walled seat (provincial city or walled town) draws exactly ONE combined bell-and-drum tower at its main street crossing (a corner of the crossing, not straddling the street), 70 ft at city tier, ~60 ft at town tier, visibly heavier-built than the skeletal fire towers; the capital tier may take the full paired gulou/zhonglou when it exists (and the street-arch platform type, 50-66 ft, is available as a garrison-city variant). **An UNWALLED town is exempt**: it has no gates to close, and its time signal is the monastery's bell - the Edo pattern, where most towns contracted a temple bell rather than building a tower - implied within the precinct, no glyph (Hoshizora). The tower follows the WALL, not the population. The caption reads **"drum/bell" over "tower"** (GM 2026-07-24): the drawn building is genuinely the combined *zhonggulou* - both instruments in one pavilion, and both are drawn - so the label says so. *Audibility - one central tower covers the whole seat with enormous margin (GM sanity check 2026-07-24, researched).* The geometry: Tango's farthest in-wall resident stands ~1,640 ft (500 m) from its tower and the farthest drawn gate-suburb structure ~790 m; Nagahara ~435 m / ~920 m. The attested anchors dwarf those distances: Edo's Kokucho time bell BILLED ~400-410 blocks of households "within earshot" (*kane-yaku-sen*, 4 mon per month per house - an effective fee radius of ~1.2-1.4 km in a far denser, noisier city, and a conservative figure, since only admitted hearers were billed); Basho casually heard the Ueno/Asakusa bells ~3-4 km away across the river; Beijing's single drum tower timed the watches for an inner city ~6.5 x 5.3 km. Extra troughs would not water anyone faster; they would just hold more standing water. What the troughs actually do is BUFFER - filled between arrivals so the first relay drinks instantly while the drawing continues - which is exactly why caravanserais centralized on one courtyard well/cistern instead of multiplying basins, and why coaching-inn yards typically show a SINGLE trough (our 2-3 is already the generous end). **The right lever if a specific map ever needs more watering throughput** (a border city where two trains overlap) is a second WELL or a cistern, never a fourth trough.
