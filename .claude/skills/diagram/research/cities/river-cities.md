## Most provincial cities sit on a river

**Grounds:** `s.river`, `meta(river_port=True)`

**Evidence:** attested, corroborated

**Sources:** not recorded - the finding is in the prose below; add a key to `SOURCES.md` when it is re-consulted

- *The history (China first, Japan agreeing).* MOST provincial cities sit on a river (Tango, on an off-map stream, is the exception). The trunk river NEVER runs *through* the walls - the one great city that let a river in, Kaifeng, silted its bed meters above the plain and was devastated by floods seven times (the 1642 flood alone killed ~300k of 378k). Instead the city stands ON one bank: the RIVER is the water defense on its flank, and the dug MOAT covers the three landward faces, tapping the river upstream and returning downstream so the current flushes it (Xiangyang's Han-river face + dug moat; Pingyao on the Fen; Okayama's Asahi "natural moat"; Shanghai's county-seat moat "connected to the Huangpu"). The ONE way water legitimately enters the walls is a **water gate** (*shuimen*) admitting a navigable CANAL, not the river - Suzhou's Pan Gate, a paired land-and-water gate with a sluiced arch, feeding the city's inner canals + a dock basin. The riverfront **wharf** (jetties, warehouses, market) sits OUTSIDE the water-side gate, the river-city version of the gate market (*guan-xiang*).

## The wharf's working face: piers, quays and stepped landings

**The GM's question (2026-08-11):** is three piers the right number for six granaries and three
warehouses, and is there something between a pier and a boardwalk - a *dock* - that the map should
be drawing? ("My guess is no on a boardwalk, because I associate that with tourism rather than an
actual wharf. But some marinas have docks and piers both. I don't know how this would have worked.")

**The boardwalk instinct is right, and for a better reason than association.** A boardwalk is a
promenade - a surface for walking along the water for its own sake. Nothing in a pre-modern working
river port is built for that. Every surface at a wharf exists to get cargo off a hull and into a
store as few times handled as possible.

**The finding, China first (Song/Ming river ports), Japan as the tiebreaker.** The dominant form is
not the pier at all: it is the **revetted quay face** - the bank itself cut back, faced with stone
or timber cribbing, and fitted with **stepped landings** at intervals. The Chinese *matou* (碼頭) is
characteristically a stone-stepped landing set into a faced bank; the Japanese riverside *kashi*
(河岸) district uses the same arrangement, with the stepped landing called *gangi* (雁木) and the
*kura* standing directly behind the bank street.

The reason is hydrology, and it is decisive: **a river's level moves through the year by many feet.**
A fixed-height deck is at the right height for a few weeks and wrong the rest of the time - too high
to reach in the dry season, awash in the wet. A flight of steps down a faced bank is at the right
height at EVERY level, because the boat simply lies against a different tread. That is why the
stepped quay is the norm on rivers and the projecting pier is the exception, and it inverts the
intuition a modern marina gives you.

**So what is the pier FOR?** Reach, when the bank shelves too gently for a loaded hull to come
alongside - the pier walks the working face out to where the water is deep enough. A port with a
steep faced bank needs few or none; a port on a shallow shelving reach needs several.

**What this means for the count.** The question "is three piers enough for nine store buildings" has
the wrong shape: the granaries and warehouses are not each served by a pier. The **bank** is the
unloading face, continuous along the frontage, and its capacity is measured in feet of usable
mooring, not in piers. A *takasebune* river barge is roughly 50-60 ft long, so a few hundred feet of
faced bank moors five or six of them abreast of the stores they are filling - which is ample for six
granaries and three warehouses, whose turnover is seasonal and dominated by the tax-rice harvest.
Three piers on top of that is not too few. It may be slightly too many for a bank this steep, but
they are within the band, and a port that also handles timber and stone wants at least one.

**Decision for the map.** Keep the three piers. What is MISSING is the thing that was actually doing
most of the work: the **faced quay edge along the granary frontage, with stepped landings at
intervals**, and mooring posts. That is a real, drawable, map-visible feature - a line of revetment
along the bank with two or three step flights notched into it - and its absence is why the wharf
currently reads as three fingers poking into an otherwise natural riverbank. No boardwalk, no
floating dock (a moored floating stage is a modern marina's answer to the same level problem, and
the steps are the pre-modern one).

**Recorded as a to-draw item**, not yet drawn: the quay face and its gangi steps need a glyph, and
the granary row's landward path should meet the step flights rather than the pier heads.
