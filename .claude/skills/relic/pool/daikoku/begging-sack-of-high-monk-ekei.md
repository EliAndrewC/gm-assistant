A mendicant's *zudabukuro*, the plain shoulder-sack a wandering monk carries his alms in: hemp cloth of the coarsest country weave, a forearm long, patched at both lower corners with cloth of a different and later brown. Three centuries of rice-dust have worked into the weave until the whole sack is the color of old tea, and it is never washed. Its mouth is gathered by a cord of black-dyed hemp, thick as a little finger, which runs through eight eyelets and is long enough to wind three times around the hand that holds it. The cord is Daikoku's color, and the color of the knot that High Monk Ekei tied in it. The sack rests in a cedar box on the high shelf of the temple's inner storehouse for all of the year except the week of the Bon Festival, and except when a country monk is sent out with it to a house that has petitioned for it.

Ekei was a monk of the Order in the Daidoji lowlands in the Hungry Year that followed the great flood, when the rice of three harvests lay rotting under silt and villages ate their seed grain and then their bark. He went from village to village with this sack on his shoulder, begging rice from houses that had a little and pouring it out in houses that had none, and in all that year it was not seen empty. What he begged at one door was always enough for the next. Houses that had sworn they had nothing would find, once Ekei was at their door, a measure put by in the rafters; and that is Daikoku's way, for plenty is never so scarce as a frightened household believes it to be, and the Fortune of wealth opens the storehouse of the one who gives.

At the Bon of that year Ekei came to a village where the dying had gone from house to house along a single lane, one household after another, as though something had learned the way. The last house on the lane still had living children in it. Ekei hung his sack open in its doorway, its mouth toward the night, and sat beside it reciting the sutras of the *segaki*, the feeding of the hungry dead, until morning. Before dawn the cord drew tight of itself across his palm. Ekei wound it three times around his hand, tied the knot, and carried the sack back to the temple heavy, though there was nothing in it that anyone could see. The children of that house lived. No one else on that lane died that year. What was in the sack the temple rolls do not say, because Ekei forbade a name to be written; the rolls call it only "a hunger that had learned the way to a house."

The teaching of the sack is the teaching of Daikoku's storehouse. Whatever reaches into Daikoku's sack may take as much as it is given, and carry that away sated. But what grasps for more than it is given will find that its hand cannot be drawn out again while it grasps. A hunger that is fed goes away content. A hunger that will not be content is held, and the cord closes on it. At the next Bon, Ekei's successor untied the knot over the segaki altar of the temple and poured rice through the sack for the hungry dead, so that what had been held might be fed at last and go on its way. Rice has been poured through it at every Bon since. It hangs open over the segaki altar for the whole of the festival week, and on those nights the lamps nearest it burn lower and bluer than the rest, the temple dogs will not lie down within sight of it, and Daikoku's rats come to sit along the shelf beneath it, as rats come to wherever their master's rice is given.

Through the rest of the year the sack is carried out to houses where one death has followed another - where a sickness has gone from a grandmother to a son to a grandchild, as though it had acquired a taste for the household. A country monk of the temple brings the sack in its box, hangs it open at the head of the sickbed for three nights, and sits up beside it reciting the segaki sutras, so that whatever hungers there may feed from Daikoku's plenty rather than from the family. Sometimes the sick recover. Sometimes they do not, and then it is understood that what was at work in that house had not yet learned to grasp, or had already been sated before the monk arrived. On a few nights in three hundred years the cord has drawn tight across a monk's palm before dawn, and the monk has tied it and carried the sack home heavy, to hang knotted in the storehouse until the next Bon. The petition is granted only by the Grand Abbot. Only a monk who has sworn the vow of devotion to Daikoku may hold the cord. Whoever carries the sack walks home by land, for by Imperial decree no relic may be carried by ship without special dispensation, lest a wreck take it to the bottom of the sea where it can never be recovered.

The sack is not to be used to snare. Ekei held a hunger only once, and only to protect a house that was being devoured; he did not hang the sack in the doorway to catch something, but to feed it, and the holding came of the hunger's own grasping. He died before the next harvest, sitting upright in meditation in his cell with the empty sack across his knees and the cord wound three times around his hand. His successors have understood that to hold a hunger is to take something of it onto oneself. And the sack must be fed. If a Bon were ever to pass without rice poured through it at the segaki altar, the hungers that have learned the way to Ekei's sack across three centuries would have to go looking for their supper elsewhere, and no monk of the temple wishes to learn where they would look.

It was for that reason that the temple refused when a beggar nun came to it pleading for the sack, so that she could carry it north to face the thing that had killed her family when she was a child. And it was carried off from the storehouse soon afterwards, by the nun and by one of the temple's own country monks, who took it the long way by land rather than break the Imperial decree. The Custodian of the storehouse has been counting the days until Bon ever since.
